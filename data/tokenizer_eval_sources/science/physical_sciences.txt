THE PHYSICAL SCIENCES: MATTER, ENERGY, AND THE STRUCTURE OF THE UNIVERSE

The physical sciences study non-living matter and the forces that act on it. Physics investigates the most general principles: motion, force, energy, fields, waves, and the constituents of matter. Chemistry investigates how atoms combine, rearrange, and transform. Astronomy applies both to objects beyond the Earth. The boundaries between them are administrative rather than natural, because the same laws govern a falling apple, a burning candle, and a collapsing star.

What unites the physical sciences is a particular working method. A phenomenon is observed, quantified, and reduced to variables. A model is proposed that predicts how those variables relate. The model is tested against measurement, and where it fails, it is modified or replaced. Models are judged by predictive accuracy, range of applicability, and economy of assumption. A model may remain useful long after a more fundamental one exists, which is why engineers still design bridges with Newtonian mechanics despite the existence of general relativity.

CLASSICAL MECHANICS: MOTION AND FORCE

Mechanics begins with kinematics, the description of motion without regard to its causes. Position specifies where an object is; displacement is the change in position, a vector quantity distinct from the scalar distance travelled. Velocity is the rate of change of position, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. For constant acceleration, four standard equations relate initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, and displacement, and they suffice for projectile motion, braking distances, and free fall.

Projectile motion illustrates a key simplification: horizontal and vertical components can be treated independently. A projectile launched horizontally falls at the same rate as one dropped from rest, because gravity acts only vertically and the horizontal velocity remains unchanged in the absence of air resistance. Maximum range for a given launch speed occurs at forty-five degrees on level ground, a result that follows directly from decomposing the motion.

Dynamics adds causes. Newton's first law states that an object maintains constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force; inertia is the tendency to resist change in motion, quantified by mass. The second law states that net force equals mass times acceleration, or more generally that force equals the rate of change of momentum. The third law states that forces occur in pairs equal in magnitude and opposite in direction acting on different bodies, which is why a rocket accelerates by expelling mass rather than by pushing against the air.

Common forces recur throughout mechanics. Weight is the gravitational force on a mass, equal to mass times gravitational field strength, about 9.81 newtons per kilogram at Earth's surface. The normal force acts perpendicular to a contact surface. Friction opposes relative sliding and is modeled as proportional to the normal force, with a static coefficient that must be exceeded to initiate motion and a usually smaller kinetic coefficient once sliding begins. Tension transmits force along ropes and cables. Drag opposes motion through a fluid and depends strongly on speed, which is why terminal velocity exists.

Circular motion requires a centripetal force directed toward the center, of magnitude mv^2/r. This force is not a new kind of force but rather whatever agency supplies it: tension for a whirling ball, friction for a cornering car, gravity for an orbiting satellite. The commonly invoked centrifugal force is not a real force but an artifact of describing motion from a rotating reference frame.

Momentum, the product of mass and velocity, is conserved in any closed system. Collision analysis uses this conservation directly. In an elastic collision kinetic energy is also conserved; in an inelastic collision it is not, though momentum still is, with the missing energy converted into deformation, heat, and sound. Impulse, the product of force and time, equals the change in momentum, which explains why airbags and crumple zones reduce injury: they extend the collision duration and thereby reduce the peak force for the same total momentum change.

Rotational mechanics parallels linear mechanics with analogous quantities. Angular displacement, angular velocity, and angular acceleration replace their linear counterparts. Torque, the product of force and lever arm, replaces force. Moment of inertia, which depends on how mass is distributed relative to the rotation axis, replaces mass. Angular momentum is conserved in the absence of external torque, which is why a spinning skater accelerates when pulling their arms inward and why gyroscopes resist reorientation.

ENERGY, WORK, AND POWER

Work is done when a force displaces an object along the direction of the force, computed as force times displacement times the cosine of the angle between them. Energy is the capacity to do work, and it appears in many forms: kinetic energy of motion equal to one half mass times velocity squared, gravitational potential energy equal to mass times gravitational field strength times height near Earth's surface, elastic potential energy stored in deformed materials, chemical energy in molecular bonds, thermal energy in random molecular motion, electrical energy, and nuclear binding energy.

The principle of conservation of energy states that energy is neither created nor destroyed, only converted between forms. A pendulum exchanges kinetic and potential energy continuously; friction gradually converts both to thermal energy, which is why real pendulums stop. Because thermal energy is disorganized, converting it back to organized mechanical work is subject to strict limits, which is the domain of thermodynamics.

Power is the rate of energy transfer, measured in watts. A device rated at one kilowatt transfers one thousand joules per second. Energy consumption is therefore power multiplied by time, which is why the kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy rather than power despite its name suggesting otherwise. Efficiency is the ratio of useful output energy to total input energy, and it is always less than one for any real device.

THERMODYNAMICS AND THERMAL PHYSICS

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. The distinction matters: a large cool object may contain more thermal energy than a small hot one, since total thermal energy depends on mass and specific heat capacity as well as temperature.

Heat transfers by three mechanisms. Conduction moves energy through direct molecular contact and is rapid in metals, where free electrons carry energy efficiently, and slow in gases and porous insulators. Convection moves energy through bulk fluid motion, driven by density differences that arise from heating. Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and requires no medium, which is how solar energy reaches Earth. The Stefan-Boltzmann law states that radiated power scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature, so hot objects radiate disproportionately more.

The zeroth law of thermodynamics establishes that thermal equilibrium is transitive, which is what makes thermometry meaningful. The first law states that the change in internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system; it is conservation of energy applied to thermal processes. The second law states that the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Entropy quantifies the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with a macroscopic state, so the second law says that systems evolve toward more probable configurations. It gives time a direction and rules out perpetual motion machines of the second kind. The third law states that entropy approaches a constant minimum as temperature approaches absolute zero, and that absolute zero cannot be reached in finite steps.

Heat engines convert thermal energy to work by moving heat from a hot reservoir to a cold one. Carnot's theorem sets the maximum possible efficiency as one minus the ratio of absolute cold to hot reservoir temperatures. This is a fundamental limit, not an engineering shortfall; it is why power plants require cooling and why waste heat is unavoidable. Refrigerators and heat pumps run the cycle in reverse, moving heat against its natural gradient at the cost of work input, and their coefficient of performance can exceed one because they move energy rather than create it.

The ideal gas law relates pressure, volume, temperature, and amount of substance as PV = nRT. It combines the earlier laws of Boyle, Charles, and Gay-Lussac and describes real gases well at moderate pressures and temperatures well above condensation. Kinetic theory derives it from mechanical assumptions about molecular collisions, connecting macroscopic thermodynamic variables to microscopic motion and providing one of the clearest examples of successful reduction between levels of description.

WAVES, SOUND, AND OPTICS

A wave transports energy without net transport of matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to their direction of travel, as with light and waves on a string. Longitudinal waves oscillate along the direction of travel, as with sound. Wavelength, frequency, amplitude, and speed characterize any wave, related by the equation that speed equals frequency times wavelength.

Wave behaviors are general across wave types. Reflection reverses direction at a boundary. Refraction bends waves when speed changes across a boundary, according to Snell's law, and it explains lenses, mirages, and the apparent bending of a stick in water. Diffraction spreads waves around obstacles and through apertures, becoming pronounced when the aperture is comparable to the wavelength, which is why sound bends around corners more readily than light. Interference combines overlapping waves, constructively where they arrive in phase and destructively where they arrive out of phase; the double-slit experiment demonstrates this and provided decisive early evidence for the wave nature of light. Standing waves form when waves reflect and interfere with themselves, producing fixed nodes and antinodes, and they determine the resonant frequencies of strings, pipes, and cavities.

The Doppler effect shifts observed frequency when source and observer move relative to one another. An approaching siren sounds higher pitched and a receding one lower. The same effect applied to light reveals the motion of stars and galaxies, and the systematic redshift of distant galaxies is a principal line of evidence for cosmic expansion.

Sound is a pressure wave in a medium, travelling at about 343 meters per second in air at room temperature and considerably faster in liquids and solids. Intensity is measured on the logarithmic decibel scale because human perception of loudness is approximately logarithmic. Resonance occurs when a driving frequency matches a natural frequency, producing large amplitude response, and it explains musical instrument function as well as certain structural failures.

Geometric optics treats light as rays. Plane mirrors form virtual images the same distance behind the surface. Concave mirrors and convex lenses converge light and can form real images that can be projected. Convex mirrors and concave lenses diverge light and form only virtual images. The thin lens equation relates object distance, image distance, and focal length, and magnification is the ratio of image to object distance. These relations account for eyeglasses, cameras, microscopes, and telescopes.

Physical optics treats light as an electromagnetic wave. Polarization describes the orientation of the oscillating electric field and is exploited in sunglasses, liquid crystal displays, and stress analysis. Thin-film interference produces the colors of soap bubbles and oil films. Diffraction gratings separate light into component wavelengths, providing the basis of spectroscopy, which is how the composition of distant stars became knowable.

ELECTRICITY AND MAGNETISM

Electric charge comes in two varieties, conventionally positive and negative, and like charges repel while unlike charges attract. Charge is conserved and quantized in multiples of the elementary charge. Coulomb's law states that the force between point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the separation, an inverse-square form it shares with gravitation.

The electric field is the force per unit charge at a point in space, a construct that allows the interaction to be described locally rather than as action at a distance. Electric potential is the potential energy per unit charge, measured in volts, and potential difference drives current in circuits. Field lines visualize the field, pointing away from positive charge and toward negative, with density indicating strength.

Current is the rate of charge flow, measured in amperes. Resistance opposes current and is measured in ohms. Ohm's law states that voltage equals current times resistance for ohmic materials. Power dissipated in a resistor equals voltage times current, equivalently current squared times resistance, which is why transmission lines operate at high voltage and low current to minimize losses.

Series circuits carry the same current through each element with voltages adding; parallel circuits apply the same voltage across each branch with currents adding. Kirchhoff's current law expresses charge conservation at junctions, and his voltage law expresses energy conservation around loops. Capacitors store energy in an electric field between conductors and oppose rapid voltage change; inductors store energy in a magnetic field and oppose rapid current change.

Magnetism arises from moving charge. A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field circling it, and permanent magnets result from aligned electron magnetic moments within a material. A charge moving through a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field, which is the basis of electric motors and mass spectrometers.

Faraday's law of induction states that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force. Lenz's law adds that the induced current opposes the change producing it, a consequence of energy conservation. Together these principles underlie generators, transformers, induction cooktops, and wireless charging. Essentially all grid electricity is produced by rotating a conductor in a magnetic field, whatever the primary energy source turning the shaft.

Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism into four equations describing how charges produce fields, how changing fields produce one another, and how the resulting disturbances propagate. The predicted propagation speed matched the measured speed of light, establishing that light is an electromagnetic wave. The electromagnetic spectrum spans radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays, differing only in wavelength and frequency while obeying identical physics.

MODERN PHYSICS

By the late nineteenth century, classical physics faced several persistent failures. The predicted spectrum of blackbody radiation diverged at short wavelengths. The photoelectric effect showed that light ejected electrons in a way depending on frequency rather than intensity. Atomic spectra consisted of discrete lines rather than continuous bands. The measured speed of light appeared independent of the observer's motion.

Quantum theory resolved the first three. Planck proposed that energy is exchanged in discrete quanta proportional to frequency. Einstein extended this to light itself, explaining the photoelectric effect through photons. Bohr applied quantization to atomic orbits, accounting for hydrogen's spectral lines. De Broglie proposed that matter also has wave properties, confirmed by electron diffraction. Schrödinger's wave equation and Heisenberg's matrix formulation gave the theory mathematical structure, describing particles by wavefunctions whose squared magnitude gives probability density.

The uncertainty principle states that certain pairs of quantities, notably position and momentum, cannot both be known precisely; the product of their uncertainties has a lower bound. This is not a limitation of instruments but a property of the objects themselves. Quantum mechanics also introduced superposition, in which a system occupies a combination of states until measured, and entanglement, in which separated particles exhibit correlations stronger than any classical model permits.

Relativity resolved the fourth failure. Special relativity begins from two postulates: the laws of physics are identical in all inertial frames, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all observers regardless of relative motion. The consequences are far-reaching. Moving clocks run slow, moving objects contract along their direction of motion, simultaneity is relative, and mass and energy are equivalent according to E = mc^2. These effects are negligible at everyday speeds but essential at high velocities, and satellite navigation systems must correct for them to maintain accuracy.

General relativity extends the theory to gravitation, describing gravity not as a force but as curvature of spacetime produced by mass and energy. It predicted the bending of starlight near the Sun, the precession of Mercury's orbit, gravitational time dilation, black holes, and gravitational waves, all subsequently observed.

The standard model of particle physics catalogs the fundamental constituents: six quarks, six leptons including the electron and neutrinos, force-carrying bosons, and the Higgs boson associated with the field that gives particles mass. It describes the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions with extraordinary precision while leaving gravity outside its framework, and it does not account for dark matter or dark energy. It is simultaneously the most successful and the most evidently incomplete theory in physics.

CHEMISTRY: ATOMS, BONDING, AND REACTION

Atoms consist of a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons. The proton count, the atomic number, determines the element. The neutron count determines the isotope. Electrons occupy orbitals grouped into shells and subshells, and chemical behavior is governed almost entirely by the outermost, or valence, electrons.

The periodic table organizes elements by atomic number, and its structure reflects electron configuration. Elements in the same group share valence electron counts and therefore similar chemistry. Periodic trends follow from the balance between nuclear attraction and electron shielding. Atomic radius decreases across a period as nuclear charge increases and increases down a group as shells are added. Ionization energy and electronegativity increase across a period and decrease down a group. These trends predict reactivity: alkali metals lose one electron readily, halogens gain one readily, and noble gases with filled shells react hardly at all.

Chemical bonds form because bonded arrangements have lower energy than separated atoms. Ionic bonding transfers electrons between atoms of very different electronegativity, producing charged ions held by electrostatic attraction in a lattice; the resulting compounds have high melting points, are brittle, and conduct when molten or dissolved. Covalent bonding shares electron pairs between atoms of similar electronegativity, producing discrete molecules or extended networks. Metallic bonding delocalizes valence electrons across a lattice of cations, which explains the conductivity, malleability, and luster of metals.

Molecular geometry follows from electron pair repulsion. Regions of electron density around a central atom arrange to maximize separation, giving linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, and octahedral arrangements, modified where lone pairs occupy positions. Geometry determines polarity, and polarity determines intermolecular forces. Hydrogen bonding, the strongest of these, explains water's anomalously high boiling point, its expansion on freezing, its surface tension, and its effectiveness as a solvent, all of which are essential to life.

Chemical reactions rearrange bonds. Equations must be balanced because atoms are conserved. Stoichiometry uses the mole, defined as Avogadro's number of entities, to relate masses to particle counts and thus to reaction ratios. Reaction types include synthesis, decomposition, single and double displacement, combustion, acid-base neutralization, and oxidation-reduction, the last defined by electron transfer and tracked with oxidation numbers.

Thermochemistry accounts for energy change in reactions. Exothermic reactions release energy because product bonds are stronger than reactant bonds; endothermic reactions absorb it. Enthalpy change is measured at constant pressure, and Hess's law allows enthalpy changes to be computed by adding steps of any convenient path, since enthalpy is a state function. Whether a reaction proceeds spontaneously depends on Gibbs free energy, which combines enthalpy and entropy with temperature, explaining why some endothermic processes such as dissolution proceed spontaneously when entropy increases sufficiently.

Kinetics addresses reaction rate rather than direction. Rates increase with concentration, temperature, and surface area, and catalysts increase rates by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy without being consumed. The Arrhenius relationship describes the strong exponential temperature dependence, which is why refrigeration preserves food and why modest warming can substantially accelerate corrosion.

Equilibrium occurs when forward and reverse rates become equal and concentrations stop changing, though both reactions continue. The equilibrium constant expresses the ratio of product to reactant concentrations at that point. Le Chatelier's principle states that a system at equilibrium responds to disturbance in the direction that partially offsets it, which guides industrial process design, notably in ammonia synthesis where pressure and temperature are chosen to balance yield against rate.

Acids donate protons and bases accept them in the Brønsted-Lowry framework. The pH scale measures hydrogen ion concentration logarithmically, running from strongly acidic near zero through neutral at seven to strongly basic near fourteen. Buffers resist pH change by containing a weak acid and its conjugate base, and they maintain the narrow pH ranges required by blood, seawater, and cellular processes.

ASTRONOMY AND COSMOLOGY

Stars form when clouds of gas and dust collapse under gravity until core temperature and pressure permit hydrogen fusion. The outward pressure of fusion balances gravitational collapse, producing a stable main sequence lasting from millions of years for massive stars to many billions for small ones. When core hydrogen is exhausted, the star expands into a giant and fuses heavier elements. Low-mass stars end as white dwarfs; massive stars explode as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes and dispersing heavy elements into space. Every element heavier than helium in ordinary matter was produced in stars or stellar explosions.

The solar system contains rocky inner planets, gas and ice giants beyond the asteroid belt, moons, comets, and the distant Kuiper belt. Planetary orbits follow Kepler's laws: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus, equal areas are swept in equal times, and the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. Newton derived these from his law of universal gravitation, which was a decisive demonstration that terrestrial and celestial mechanics are the same subject.

Galaxies contain billions of stars bound by gravity, and they cluster into groups and superclusters separated by vast voids. Observations of galactic rotation show that visible matter is insufficient to explain the observed dynamics, implying substantial dark matter. Observations of distant supernovae show that cosmic expansion is accelerating, attributed to dark energy. Together these constitute most of the mass-energy content of the universe and remain unexplained.

The Big Bang model describes an expanding universe originating from an extremely hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago. Three independent lines of evidence support it: the redshift of distant galaxies indicating expansion, the cosmic microwave background radiation as relic heat from the era when the universe first became transparent, and the observed abundances of light elements matching predictions of primordial nucleosynthesis. The model describes the evolution of the universe from a fraction of a second onward without claiming to describe its origin, a distinction frequently blurred in popular accounts.

THE CHARACTER OF PHYSICAL KNOWLEDGE

The physical sciences advance by successive approximation rather than by accumulation of final truths. Newtonian mechanics was not discarded when relativity arrived; it was recognized as the low-velocity, weak-field limit of a more general theory, and it remains correct within that domain. Classical thermodynamics was not invalidated by statistical mechanics; it was explained by it. This pattern of subsuming rather than discarding is the normal mode of progress, and it explains why older theories retain practical utility.

Measurement discipline underlies everything. Every quantity requires units, and the international system defines seven base units from which all others derive. Significant figures communicate precision, and a computed result cannot be more precise than its least precise input. Uncertainty must be stated and propagated. Systematic errors bias results consistently and are not reduced by repetition; random errors scatter and are reduced by averaging. A measurement without an uncertainty is not a measurement but an assertion, and much of the reliability of physical science comes from taking that principle seriously.
