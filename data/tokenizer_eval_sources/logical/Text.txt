# The Architecture of Reasoning

### An Essay on Deduction, Logic, Constraint, Pattern, Quantity, Chance, and Algorithm

---

## I. Prologue: Why Reasoning Has a Structure

There is a temptation to treat reasoning as a single undifferentiated talent — the thing that "smart people have more of." This is false in an interesting way. Reasoning is not one faculty but a federation of related disciplines, each with its own vocabulary, its own characteristic errors, its own criteria for success, and its own domain of applicability. A person can be excellent at manipulating truth tables and terrible at estimating probabilities. A person can solve a Sudoku without hesitation and still fall for the conjunction fallacy. An engineer who designs flawless combinational logic circuits may nonetheless reason poorly about the reliability of a system that contains them, because reliability is a probabilistic question and gate-level logic is a deterministic one.

This essay is an attempt to lay out the federation. It moves from the most rigid and mechanical forms of inference — deduction, where the conclusion is contained in the premises with the airtightness of a mathematical identity — through progressively looser and more world-facing forms: conditional reasoning, Boolean algebra, categorical syllogism, constraint satisfaction, pattern extrapolation, quantitative modeling, probabilistic inference, algorithmic construction, and finally the metacognitive discipline of step-by-step problem solving, which is less a subject than a habit that binds the others together.

Two threads run through everything that follows.

The first thread is the distinction between **validity** and **truth**. Validity is a property of the *form* of an argument: given these premises, must this conclusion follow? Truth is a property of the *content*: does this statement correspond to how things actually are? A valid argument from false premises can deliver a false conclusion without any logical error having occurred. An invalid argument can stumble onto a true conclusion by luck. Almost every catastrophic reasoning failure in engineering, in policy, and in ordinary life comes from confusing these two — from treating the internal coherence of a model as evidence that the model describes reality, or conversely from rejecting a valid derivation because its conclusion feels wrong.

The second thread is the distinction between **what is guaranteed** and **what is merely supported**. Deduction guarantees. Induction, abduction, statistical inference, and pattern extrapolation only support. The strength of that support is often enormous — no sane person doubts that the sun will rise tomorrow — but the logical status is categorically different, and the difference matters at exactly the moments when it is most tempting to ignore. When a sequence of five data points fits a straight line, the line is a hypothesis, not a theorem. When a test comes back positive, the disease is more likely, not certain. Keeping the modality of a conclusion attached to the conclusion itself — *this follows necessarily* versus *this is the best current explanation* versus *this is one of several fits* — is perhaps the single most valuable reasoning habit a person can cultivate.

What follows is organized as an ascent in one respect and a descent in another. It ascends in generality: each section handles a wider class of problem than the last. It descends in certainty: each section trades away some of the guarantee that deduction offers in exchange for the ability to say something about a messier world.

## II. Deductive Reasoning: The Machinery of Necessity

### The core definition

An argument is **deductively valid** if and only if it is impossible for all of its premises to be true while its conclusion is false. Note what this definition does *not* say. It does not say the premises are true. It does not say the conclusion is true. It does not say the premises are relevant, interesting, or well chosen. It makes a claim purely about the relationship between premise-set and conclusion, and that claim is modal: it concerns impossibility, not mere improbability.

An argument is **sound** if it is valid *and* all of its premises are in fact true. Soundness is what we actually want; validity is what logic alone can certify. Logic is the study of the form of inference, and forms are indifferent to content. This indifference is not a defect. It is precisely what makes formal logic transferable — the same schema that governs "all humans are mortal" governs "all inputs to this function are non-null," and the fact that the machinery does not care about the subject matter is why it can be mechanized in a theorem prover or a compiler.

Consider:

> All Venezuelan cities are located on Mars.
> Caracas is a Venezuelan city.
> Therefore, Caracas is located on Mars.

This is perfectly valid and grotesquely unsound. The reasoning is impeccable; the first premise is false. Contrast:

> Some metals conduct electricity.
> Copper is a metal.
> Therefore, copper conducts electricity.

Both premises are true and the conclusion is true, yet the argument is *invalid*. "Some metals conduct" does not license the inference to any particular metal. The conclusion happens to be true for reasons the argument does not supply. This is the more insidious failure, because a true conclusion masks a broken inference, and the broken inference will produce a false conclusion the next time it is applied to different content.

### The canonical valid forms

A small number of argument schemas recur so often that they have names. In what follows, `P`, `Q`, and `R` stand for arbitrary propositions.

**Modus ponens** (the mode that affirms):
> If P then Q. P. Therefore Q.

**Modus tollens** (the mode that denies):
> If P then Q. Not Q. Therefore not P.

**Hypothetical syllogism** (chaining):
> If P then Q. If Q then R. Therefore if P then R.

**Disjunctive syllogism** (elimination):
> P or Q. Not P. Therefore Q.

**Constructive dilemma**:
> If P then Q. If R then S. P or R. Therefore Q or S.

**Reductio ad absurdum** (proof by contradiction):
> Assume P. Derive a contradiction. Therefore not P.

**Conjunction introduction and elimination**:
> P. Q. Therefore P and Q. — and — P and Q. Therefore P.

**Universal instantiation**:
> Everything has property F. Therefore this particular thing has property F.

These forms are the load-bearing beams of every proof ever written. It is worth internalizing them to the point where recognizing them requires no effort, because the corresponding *invalid* forms are superficially almost identical.

### The canonical invalid forms

**Affirming the consequent**:
> If P then Q. Q. Therefore P.

If it rained, the street is wet. The street is wet. Therefore it rained. But a burst pipe, a street cleaner, or a neighbor's hose would also do it. The conditional licenses travel from antecedent to consequent, not the reverse. This fallacy is the engine behind an enormous amount of bad diagnostic reasoning: "if the capacitor were failing, we'd see this ripple; we see this ripple; therefore the capacitor is failing." The ripple is *consistent* with the hypothesis; consistency is not confirmation, because the observation may be consistent with a dozen other hypotheses as well.

**Denying the antecedent**:
> If P then Q. Not P. Therefore not Q.

If you study, you will pass. You did not study. Therefore you will not pass. But the conditional never claimed studying was the *only* route to passing. It claimed it was sufficient, not necessary.

**The undistributed middle**:
> All A are B. All C are B. Therefore all A are C.

All engineers are problem-solvers. All chess players are problem-solvers. Therefore all engineers are chess players. The shared category B is too coarse to connect A and C.

**Illicit conversion of a universal**:
> All A are B. Therefore all B are A.

All squares are rectangles; not all rectangles are squares. This one is easy to catch in geometry and remarkably hard to catch in politics, where "all X are Y" and "all Y are X" get conflated constantly.

### Deduction and knowledge

A philosophically loaded question hovers over all of this: if the conclusion of a valid deduction is already contained in the premises, does deduction ever teach us anything? The answer is that "contained in" does a lot of concealed work. Every theorem of arithmetic is contained in the Peano axioms in the sense that it follows from them, but Fermat's Last Theorem took three and a half centuries to extract. Deduction is not information-creating in the semantic sense, but it is overwhelmingly information-*revealing* in the practical sense. The limits of what a finite mind can unpack from a small set of premises are severe, and closing the gap between what is implied and what is known is most of what mathematics does.

This has a direct engineering analogue. A circuit's behavior is fully determined by its schematic and component values. Everything is "contained in" the design. And yet nobody can look at a schematic and simply *see* the transient response; you simulate, you measure, you derive. The determination is total and the transparency is nil. Deductive systems are the same: total determination, negligible transparency, and the entire discipline exists to convert the first into the second.

## III. Conditional Logic: The Most Useful and Most Slippery Connective

### The material conditional

The statement "if P then Q," written `P → Q`, is the workhorse of formal reasoning and the source of more confusion than every other connective combined. In classical logic it is defined truth-functionally: `P → Q` is false in exactly one case, when `P` is true and `Q` is false. In all three other cases it is true.

| P | Q | P → Q |
|---|---|-------|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

The last two rows generate the trouble. They say that a conditional with a false antecedent is automatically true — "if the moon is made of cheese, then I am the king of Spain" is a *true* statement in classical logic. This is called **vacuous truth**, and it strikes most people as absurd on first contact.

The absurdity dissolves once you see what the material conditional is for. It is not a claim about causation, relevance, or explanation. It is a claim about the *absence of a specific counterexample*. `P → Q` asserts precisely: "there is no case where P holds and Q fails." If P never holds, there is trivially no such case, so the assertion stands unrefuted. A rule like "all employees who submit expenses before the 5th are reimbursed that month" is not violated by an employee who submits nothing.

Vacuous truth is not a philosophical curiosity; it is a practical hazard in specification and testing. A requirement of the form "if the sensor reports an overtemperature, the system shall enter safe mode" is satisfied by a system whose sensor never reports anything at all. Every test suite that only checks conditionals with the antecedent false is passing vacuously. This is why coverage metrics distinguish condition coverage from branch coverage, and why a specification written entirely in implications needs an accompanying liveness or reachability claim: something must actually make the antecedents true, or the whole specification is satisfied by a brick.

### Necessary and sufficient conditions

`P → Q` can be read in two directions and both readings matter:

- P is a **sufficient** condition for Q. Having P is enough to get Q.
- Q is a **necessary** condition for P. Without Q, you cannot have P.

The second reading is just the contrapositive: `P → Q` is logically equivalent to `¬Q → ¬P`. Contraposition is the most reliable tool in conditional reasoning, and it is one of very few transformations that preserves meaning exactly.

The **converse** `Q → P` and the **inverse** `¬P → ¬Q` are *not* equivalent to the original. They are equivalent to each other, which is a neat symmetry, but neither follows from `P → Q`. Confusing a statement with its converse is the single most common conditional error in natural language, largely because ordinary speech often intends a **biconditional** while uttering a conditional. "If you finish your work, you can go out" is usually meant as "if and only if" — the speaker intends both that finishing suffices and that not finishing forbids. Formal contexts cannot afford this looseness. A specification that says "if the checksum matches, accept the packet" has not said what to do when it does not match, and an implementer who assumes the converse has invented a requirement.

The biconditional `P ↔ Q` is true exactly when P and Q have the same truth value. It is equivalent to `(P → Q) ∧ (Q → P)`, and proving one requires proving both halves — a discipline that catches an enormous number of subtle errors in mathematical writing, where "iff" is asserted far more often than it is fully demonstrated.

### Nested and chained conditionals

Conditionals compose, but not always in intuitive ways.

`P → (Q → R)` is equivalent to `(P ∧ Q) → R`. This is **exportation**, and it is the formal justification for the everyday practice of accumulating assumptions: to prove "if P then if Q then R," you may simply assume both P and Q and prove R.

`(P → Q) → R` is *not* equivalent to `P → (Q → R)`. Implication is not associative. The outer conditional in the first has a conditional as its antecedent, which means it is triggered by the *truth of a rule*, not by the truth of a fact — a very different and much rarer claim.

Chaining is safe: from `P → Q` and `Q → R`, `P → R` follows. This is what allows long derivations to be assembled from short steps, and it is why proof is possible at all. But chains only run forward. From `P → Q` and `R → Q` nothing about P and R follows.

### Counterfactuals and the limits of the material conditional

Natural language conditionals frequently mean something the material conditional cannot capture. "If I had left ten minutes earlier, I would have caught the train" is a **counterfactual**: its antecedent is known false, so as a material conditional it is vacuously true and therefore useless. Yet counterfactuals are clearly not all equally true — "if I had left ten minutes earlier, I would have become emperor of Japan" is false in a way the first is not.

Handling this requires a possible-worlds semantics: a counterfactual is true if, in the closest possible world where the antecedent holds, the consequent also holds. "Closest" does heavy lifting and is not fully formalizable, which is why counterfactual reasoning remains genuinely hard. It is also indispensable — root-cause analysis is counterfactual reasoning ("would the failure have occurred if this component had been within spec?"), and so is nearly all causal inference. When you find yourself reasoning about what would have happened, you have left the territory of truth tables and entered something requiring judgment about which alternative scenarios are relevant.

### Indicative conditionals and probability

There is a third reading, distinct from both the material and the counterfactual: the **indicative conditional** as a statement about conditional probability. "If it's cloudy tomorrow, it will probably rain" is not asserting the absence of a counterexample; it is asserting that P(rain | cloudy) is high. Much of ordinary conditional talk is of this kind, which is why people's intuitions about `→` diverge so sharply from the truth table. When you notice this divergence in a discussion, the productive move is usually to ask which conditional is meant, because arguing about the truth of an ambiguous conditional is arguing about two different propositions at once.

## IV. Boolean Logic: Algebra for Truth

### From connectives to algebra

Boolean logic takes the propositional connectives and treats them as operations in an algebraic structure. The values are `0` and `1` (false and true). The operations are conjunction (`∧`, AND, often written as multiplication), disjunction (`∨`, OR, often written as addition), and negation (`¬`, NOT, often written with an overbar or prime). What makes this an algebra rather than a mere notation is that these operations obey laws, and the laws license rewriting — which is to say, simplification.

The laws:

- **Identity**: `A ∧ 1 = A`, `A ∨ 0 = A`
- **Domination** (annihilation): `A ∧ 0 = 0`, `A ∨ 1 = 1`
- **Idempotence**: `A ∧ A = A`, `A ∨ A = A`
- **Complement**: `A ∧ ¬A = 0`, `A ∨ ¬A = 1`
- **Double negation**: `¬¬A = A`
- **Commutativity**: `A ∧ B = B ∧ A`, `A ∨ B = B ∨ A`
- **Associativity**: `(A ∧ B) ∧ C = A ∧ (B ∧ C)`, similarly for `∨`
- **Distributivity**: `A ∧ (B ∨ C) = (A ∧ B) ∨ (A ∧ C)` and, unlike ordinary arithmetic, also `A ∨ (B ∧ C) = (A ∨ B) ∧ (A ∨ C)`
- **Absorption**: `A ∨ (A ∧ B) = A`, `A ∧ (A ∨ B) = A`
- **De Morgan's laws**: `¬(A ∧ B) = ¬A ∨ ¬B`, `¬(A ∨ B) = ¬A ∧ ¬B`

The double distributivity is worth pausing on, because it is where Boolean algebra departs from the arithmetic most people learned first. In the reals, `a + (b × c) ≠ (a + b) × (a + c)`. In Boolean algebra the analogous identity holds. This **duality** — every valid identity remains valid when you swap `∧` with `∨` and `0` with `1` — is a structural feature of the whole system and a free source of theorems: prove one, get its dual at no cost.

### De Morgan's laws as a reasoning tool

De Morgan's laws deserve special emphasis because they govern how negation interacts with structure, and negation is where human reasoning reliably breaks down.

"It is not the case that the device is both charged and connected" means "the device is either not charged or not connected." "It is not the case that the device is either charged or connected" means "the device is neither charged nor connected." People flip these constantly. In requirements documents, in conditional guard clauses, in database queries, in legal drafting, the negation of a compound condition is a systematic source of defects.

The practical rule: **to negate, flip every operator and negate every operand.** `¬(A ∧ ¬B ∧ C)` becomes `¬A ∨ B ∨ ¬C`. Applied mechanically, this never fails. Applied by intuition, it fails often enough to be dangerous.

De Morgan also explains functional completeness. Because `A ∨ B = ¬(¬A ∧ ¬B)`, disjunction is definable from conjunction and negation. Every Boolean function can be built from `{AND, NOT}`, or from `{OR, NOT}`, or from NAND alone, or from NOR alone. That last fact — that a single two-input gate type suffices for all of digital computation — is the reason NAND gates are the fundamental building block of CMOS logic. Universality is a logical result before it is a manufacturing one.

### Normal forms

Any Boolean expression can be rewritten in canonical shapes.

**Disjunctive normal form (DNF)** is an OR of ANDs: `(A ∧ ¬B) ∨ (¬A ∧ C) ∨ (B ∧ C)`. Each conjunct is a term describing one family of satisfying assignments. DNF corresponds directly to reading the `1` rows off a truth table: for each row where the function is true, write the AND of the literals matching that row; OR all of them together. This is the **sum of products** form, and it maps onto a two-level AND-OR gate network.

**Conjunctive normal form (CNF)** is an AND of ORs: `(A ∨ B) ∧ (¬A ∨ C)`. Each clause is a constraint that must be satisfied. CNF corresponds to reading off the `0` rows and negating. This is the **product of sums** form, and it is the input format for SAT solvers, resolution theorem provers, and most automated reasoning tools — because a clause set is naturally interpreted as a conjunction of requirements, and requirements are how problems are usually stated.

Both forms are canonical in the sense that every function has one, but neither is unique or minimal without further constraints, and the conversion between them can cause exponential blowup in expression size. This is not a defect of the algorithms; it reflects a real asymmetry in how information is packaged.

### Minimization

Two expressions can denote the same function while differing enormously in size, and in hardware size means area, power, and propagation delay. Minimization is therefore a first-class concern.

The algebraic approach applies the laws above until no further reduction is apparent. Consider `F = A∧B ∨ A∧¬B ∨ ¬A∧B`. Factor the first two: `A∧(B ∨ ¬B) = A∧1 = A`. So `F = A ∨ (¬A∧B)`. Now apply the absorption-like identity `A ∨ (¬A ∧ B) = A ∨ B`. Result: `F = A ∨ B`. Three three-literal terms collapse to a single two-input OR gate.

The **Karnaugh map** systematizes this for small functions. Variables are arranged so that adjacent cells differ in exactly one variable — a Gray-code ordering — which means adjacent `1` cells can always be merged, eliminating the variable that changes. Groups must be rectangular and of size a power of two; the map wraps around at the edges; and the goal is to cover every `1` with as few and as large groups as possible. The elegance of the K-map is that it makes the algebraic identity `X∧Y ∨ X∧¬Y = X` *visually obvious* rather than something you have to notice. It is a representation change that converts a symbolic search into a geometric one, and it is a small masterpiece of the general principle that the right notation does much of the reasoning for you.

Beyond four or five variables, K-maps become unwieldy and the Quine–McCluskey algorithm takes over: systematically generate all prime implicants, then solve a covering problem to select a minimal subset. That covering problem is itself NP-hard, which is a useful early warning that even the tidiest corner of logic runs into computational walls.

**Don't-care conditions** deserve mention. When certain input combinations cannot occur — a BCD decoder never sees `1010` through `1111` — the output for those inputs is unconstrained, and may be assigned whichever value makes the grouping larger. Exploiting don't-cares often halves the gate count. The general lesson transfers well beyond circuits: knowing which parts of a problem you are *not* required to handle is as valuable as knowing which parts you are.

### Boolean reasoning outside circuits

The same algebra governs search queries, database predicates, access control rules, type systems, and set operations, where `∧` becomes intersection, `∨` becomes union, and `¬` becomes complement. De Morgan's laws are the same laws. Distributivity is the same distributivity. When a filter expression in a data pipeline behaves unexpectedly, the diagnosis is almost always a Boolean one: an operator precedence assumption, a mishandled negation, or an implicit conversion of a three-valued (true/false/null) logic into a two-valued one. That last case is worth flagging: SQL's `NULL` breaks the law of excluded middle, so `A ∨ ¬A` is not guaranteed true, and reasoning that silently assumes classical logic will produce wrong results. Knowing which logic you are in is a precondition for reasoning correctly within it.

## V. Truth Tables: Exhaustive Semantics and Its Price

### What a truth table actually is

A truth table is a complete enumeration of the possible assignments of truth values to the atomic propositions in an expression, together with the resulting value of the expression under each assignment. For `n` atoms there are `2^n` rows. The table is a *semantic* object: it does not derive anything, it simply displays everything.

This exhaustiveness is the truth table's superpower and its curse. Superpower, because it settles questions with total finality and requires no cleverness whatsoever — a truth table cannot fail to notice a case, cannot be swayed by plausibility, and can be executed by a machine or a bored undergraduate with equal reliability. Curse, because `2^n` grows in the way it grows: ten propositions give a thousand rows, twenty give a million, sixty-four give more rows than there are grains of sand on Earth.

### Constructing one correctly

The mechanics are worth stating precisely, because most truth-table errors are bookkeeping errors rather than conceptual ones.

1. Identify every distinct atomic proposition. Distinctness matters: `P` and `¬P` are not two atoms.
2. Create `2^n` rows. Use a systematic ordering — the standard is to let the leftmost variable alternate in blocks of `2^(n-1)`, the next in blocks of `2^(n-2)`, down to the rightmost alternating every row. This is just counting in binary, and adopting it means you can verify completeness at a glance.
3. Add a column for each *subexpression*, working from the innermost outward. Do not attempt to compute a complex expression in one step. The intermediate columns are where errors are caught.
4. Compute the final column.

The intermediate-column discipline is the whole game. A person who writes only the final column is doing arithmetic in their head under conditions designed to produce mistakes; a person who writes each subexpression is doing a mechanical lookup at each step.

### Classification of expressions

Once a table is built, the expression falls into exactly one of three classes.

A **tautology** is true in every row. `P ∨ ¬P` is the archetype. Tautologies are the logical truths — true by form alone, carrying no information about the world. Every theorem of propositional logic is a tautology.

A **contradiction** is false in every row. `P ∧ ¬P`. Contradictions are unsatisfiable; from a contradiction, classically, anything follows (*ex falso quodlibet*), which is why an inconsistent specification is not merely partially wrong but wholly vacuous — it licenses every conclusion and therefore constrains nothing.

A **contingency** is true in some rows and false in others. Every statement that actually says something about the world is a contingency. This is not a hierarchy of quality: tautologies are useful precisely because they are unconditionally available as inference steps, and contingencies are useful precisely because they discriminate between possibilities.

### Testing validity with truth tables

To test whether a conclusion `C` follows from premises `P₁ … Pₙ`, build the table for all of them and look for a **counterexample row**: a row where every premise is true and the conclusion is false. If no such row exists, the argument is valid. If one exists, you have not merely shown invalidity — you have produced a concrete, exhibitable counterexample, which is far more persuasive than an abstract claim of invalidity.

Equivalently, the argument is valid iff `(P₁ ∧ … ∧ Pₙ) → C` is a tautology, and iff `P₁ ∧ … ∧ Pₙ ∧ ¬C` is a contradiction. This last formulation is the basis of refutation-style automated reasoning: to prove something, assume its negation alongside the premises and derive unsatisfiability. It is the same move as proof by contradiction, mechanized.

### The shortcut method

Full tables are wasteful when you only need a yes-or-no answer. The **shortcut** or **truth-tree** method works backwards: *assume* the argument is invalid, which forces the conclusion false and all premises true, and see whether those forced assignments can be consistently completed.

Test `((P → Q) ∧ (Q → R)) → (P → R)`:

Assume the whole thing false. A conditional is false only when antecedent is true and consequent false. So `(P → Q) ∧ (Q → R)` is true and `P → R` is false. From the second: `P = T`, `R = F`. From the first conjunct being true: `P → Q` is true and `P = T`, so `Q = T`. But then `Q → R` requires `T → F`, which is false — contradicting the assumption that both conjuncts were true. No consistent assignment exists. The formula is a tautology; hypothetical syllogism is valid.

That derivation touched perhaps six facts. The full table would have had eight rows and six columns. On larger formulas the saving is the difference between feasible and infeasible.

### The combinatorial wall and what lies beyond it

The exponential blowup is not a shortcoming of the technique; it is a shadow of a deep fact. Determining whether a propositional formula has *any* satisfying assignment — the **Boolean satisfiability problem**, SAT — was the first problem proved NP-complete, by Cook and Levin in 1971. If a genuinely efficient (polynomial-time) algorithm exists for SAT, then one exists for every problem in NP, and the P vs NP question is settled in the affirmative. Nobody believes this, and nobody can prove it false.

Yet modern SAT solvers routinely dispatch instances with millions of variables. The resolution of this apparent paradox is instructive and applies far beyond logic: worst-case complexity is a statement about the hardest instances in a class, not about the instances people actually encounter. Real-world formulas have structure — locality, modularity, near-decomposability — and algorithms like conflict-driven clause learning exploit that structure aggressively. The general lesson for any reasoner is that "this problem is intractable" is a claim about a *class*, and the instance in front of you may be nothing like the worst member of its class. Conversely, a technique that works beautifully on your instances offers no guarantee about the next one.

### Beyond two values

Classical truth tables assume bivalence: every proposition is exactly one of true or false. Several important systems relax this.

**Three-valued logics** (Kleene, Łukasiewicz) add a third value for undefined, unknown, or not-yet-computed. Kleene's strong three-valued logic is the semantics behind SQL's `NULL` handling and behind hardware description languages where a signal may be `X` (unknown) or `Z` (high-impedance). In these systems, `A ∨ ¬A` is not a tautology when `A` is unknown, and reasoning that assumes excluded middle silently is reasoning in the wrong system.

**Fuzzy logic** allows a continuum of truth values in `[0,1]`, with conjunction as minimum and disjunction as maximum. It is a model of vagueness, not of uncertainty — "this water is hot" is a matter of degree, which is different from "I am unsure whether this water is hot." Conflating vagueness with probability is a persistent category error.

**Intuitionistic logic** rejects the law of excluded middle for a different reason: it treats truth as constructive provability, so `A ∨ ¬A` requires either a proof of `A` or a proof of `¬A`, and for many propositions we have neither. Its practical importance is enormous in computer science, where the Curry–Howard correspondence identifies intuitionistic proofs with programs and propositions with types. A double-negation elimination is precisely the step that a constructive system disallows and that a program cannot perform, because "not not having a value" does not hand you a value.

## VI. Syllogisms: The Logic of Categories

### Categorical propositions

Aristotelian syllogistic predates propositional logic by two millennia and handles something propositional logic cannot: the internal structure of statements about classes. Its atoms are four forms, traditionally labeled with vowels from the Latin *AffIrmo* and *nEgO*:

- **A** — Universal affirmative: *All S are P.*
- **E** — Universal negative: *No S are P.*
- **I** — Particular affirmative: *Some S are P.*
- **O** — Particular negative: *Some S are not P.*

Each relates a **subject** term `S` and a **predicate** term `P`. A syllogism combines two such premises sharing a **middle term** `M`, yielding a conclusion linking the remaining two terms.

### Figures and moods

The **mood** is the triple of forms, e.g. AAA or EIO. The **figure** is the arrangement of the middle term:

- Figure 1: `M–P`, `S–M` → `S–P`
- Figure 2: `P–M`, `S–M` → `S–P`
- Figure 3: `M–P`, `M–S` → `S–P`
- Figure 4: `P–M`, `M–S` → `S–P`

Four figures times sixty-four moods gives 256 possible syllogisms, of which twenty-four are valid (fifteen without assuming existential import; nine more with it). Medieval logicians memorized them with mnemonic names — *Barbara*, *Celarent*, *Darii*, *Ferio*, *Cesare*, *Camestres*, *Festino*, *Baroco*, *Darapti*, and so on, where the vowels encode the mood.

*Barbara* (AAA-1) is the archetype:
> All M are P. All S are M. Therefore all S are P.

*Ferio* (EIO-1):
> No M are P. Some S are M. Therefore some S are not P.

### The rules of validity

Rather than memorize twenty-four cases, one can apply five rules. Violating any one makes the syllogism invalid.

**Rule 1 — The middle term must be distributed at least once.** A term is *distributed* in a proposition if the proposition says something about every member of that class. Subjects are distributed in A and E; predicates are distributed in E and O. Violating this rule gives the fallacy of the undistributed middle.

**Rule 2 — A term distributed in the conclusion must be distributed in its premise.** You cannot conclude something about all members of a class from a premise that spoke only of some. Violations are *illicit major* and *illicit minor*.

**Rule 3 — Two negative premises yield no conclusion.** If `M` is excluded from both `S` and `P`, nothing follows about how `S` and `P` relate.

**Rule 4 — If either premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative; and a negative conclusion requires a negative premise.** Quality must match.

**Rule 5 — Two universal premises cannot yield a particular conclusion** (under the modern, non-existential-import reading). This is where the count drops from twenty-four to fifteen.

The distribution concept is genuinely subtle and repays careful attention. In "All dogs are mammals," the subject *dogs* is distributed — the claim covers every dog — but the predicate *mammals* is not, since the claim says nothing about mammals generally. In "No dogs are reptiles," both terms are distributed: the claim separates every dog from every reptile. Once distribution is internalized, most syllogistic errors become visible immediately.

### Venn diagrams

A far more reliable method than rule-checking is diagramming. Draw three overlapping circles for `S`, `M`, `P`. Diagram the *universal* premises first, by shading regions asserted to be empty. Then diagram the particular premises by placing an `×` in a region asserted to be non-empty, putting the `×` on a boundary if the premise does not determine which side it falls on. Finally, read the diagram: the syllogism is valid if and only if the conclusion is already depicted, without any further mark being needed.

The order matters. Shading first eliminates regions, which frequently forces an `×` that would otherwise be ambiguous into a determinate position. Diagramming particulars first loses that information.

Venn diagrams work because they are a faithful spatial model of the semantics, and they exemplify a broader principle already seen with Karnaugh maps: converting a symbolic problem into a spatial one recruits perceptual machinery that is fast, parallel, and hard to fool. Whenever a reasoning task has a natural diagrammatic representation, building the diagram is almost always cheaper than reasoning symbolically, and it produces artifacts that others can check.

### Existential import

A genuine dispute lurks here. Does "All unicorns are white" imply that unicorns exist? Aristotle assumed yes; modern predicate logic says no, rendering the A form as `∀x(Ux → Wx)`, which is vacuously true if nothing is a unicorn.

The modern reading is chosen because it makes universal statements usable in mathematics — "every even prime greater than two is composite" is comfortably true and comfortably about nothing. But it invalidates nine traditional syllogisms, notably *Darapti* (AAI-3):
> All M are P. All M are S. Therefore some S are P.

Under the modern reading this fails when `M` is empty. Under the traditional reading it is valid because `M` was presumed non-empty. Neither reading is wrong; they answer different questions. The practical lesson is that "valid" is always relative to a semantics, and disagreements about validity are often disagreements about interpretation masquerading as disagreements about logic.

### Sorites and the limits of the form

Chained syllogisms — **sorites** — extend the machinery: a series of premises, each sharing a term with the next, collapsing into a single conclusion. Lewis Carroll constructed elaborate and deliberately absurd examples ("no kitten that loves fish is unteachable...") precisely to demonstrate that the mechanism is content-blind.

Syllogistic's real limitation is that it cannot handle relations. "All horses are animals, therefore all heads of horses are heads of animals" is obviously valid and entirely beyond Aristotelian resources, because it involves a two-place relation *head of*. Frege's predicate logic, with quantifiers and n-place predicates, subsumes syllogistic entirely and handles relations, nested quantification, and identity. The syllogism survives as a pedagogical device and as an accurate model of a particular slice of ordinary reasoning, but as a general logic it was superseded in 1879.

Nested quantification is where the real difficulty starts, and it is worth a paragraph. `∀x ∃y (Loves(x,y))` — everyone loves someone — is quite different from `∃y ∀x (Loves(x,y))` — someone is loved by everyone. The second implies the first; the first does not imply the second. Every specification of the form "for every request there exists a handler" versus "there exists a handler for every request" turns on this distinction, and getting the quantifier order wrong is a classic source of both mathematical error and mis-specified systems. The epsilon-delta definition of a limit is hard for students almost entirely because of quantifier alternation, not because of any difficulty with the inequalities.

## VII. Constraint Problems: Reasoning Under Simultaneous Restrictions

### The formalization

A **constraint satisfaction problem** (CSP) is a triple `⟨X, D, C⟩`:

- `X = {x₁, …, xₙ}` — a set of variables
- `D = {D₁, …, Dₙ}` — a domain of candidate values for each variable
- `C` — a set of constraints, each restricting the allowed combinations of values over some subset of variables

A **solution** is an assignment of one value from each domain to each variable such that every constraint is satisfied.

This framing is startlingly general. Sudoku, graph coloring, timetabling, register allocation in compilers, circuit layout, crew scheduling, the classic zebra puzzle, cryptarithms, and n-queens are all CSPs. So is much of everyday planning. Recognizing that a mess of interlocking requirements *is* a CSP is often the entire insight; once it is formalized, standard machinery applies.

### The zebra-puzzle pattern

Grid logic puzzles — five houses, five nationalities, five drinks, five pets, five colors, with clues like "the Norwegian lives next to the blue house" — are the purest recreational instance. The canonical method is a grid where rows are attributes and columns are positions, marked with `✓` for confirmed and `✗` for eliminated.

Two inference patterns do nearly all the work:

**Elimination**: if a value is assigned to one variable, remove it from the domains of all variables that must differ from it. This is constraint propagation in its simplest form.

**Forced placement** (the pigeonhole move): if a value has been eliminated from all but one position, it must go there. This is the dual of elimination and is the step that beginners most often miss, because it requires scanning a row for absences rather than presences — and absence is harder to perceive than presence.

The discipline that separates fast solvers from slow ones is not cleverness but bookkeeping: recording every consequence of every deduction immediately, rather than holding partial conclusions in working memory. Working memory is the bottleneck, and externalizing state onto paper removes the bottleneck almost entirely. This is the same principle as writing intermediate columns in a truth table, and it generalizes: *the reasoner's job is to make deductions; the paper's job is to remember them.*

### Consistency and propagation

Formal CSP algorithms systematize these moves.

**Node consistency** enforces unary constraints — trim each domain to values satisfying constraints on that variable alone.

**Arc consistency** enforces binary constraints. An arc `(xᵢ, xⱼ)` is consistent if, for every value in `Dᵢ`, there is at least one value in `Dⱼ` satisfying the constraint between them. Values with no support are deleted, which may destroy support elsewhere, so deletions cascade. The AC-3 algorithm maintains a worklist of arcs to re-examine and runs to a fixed point. If any domain empties, the problem is unsatisfiable; if every domain reduces to a singleton, the propagation alone has solved it.

Many well-constructed logic puzzles are solvable by propagation alone, without any guessing. Sudoku puzzles rated "easy" are precisely those; "hard" ones require search.

**Path consistency** and higher-order `k`-consistency extend this further, at increasing cost. There is a trade-off curve here that recurs throughout algorithmics: stronger inference at each node means fewer nodes explored, but more work per node. The optimum is problem-dependent, and finding it empirically rather than dogmatically is the mark of a mature practitioner.

### Backtracking search and its refinements

When propagation stalls, search resumes. Naive backtracking assigns a value, propagates, and on failure undoes the assignment and tries the next value. Several heuristics transform its performance:

**Minimum remaining values (MRV)**: choose the variable with the smallest remaining domain. This is the "most constrained variable first" principle, and it fails fast — if a branch is doomed, you want to discover that at depth two rather than depth twenty.

**Degree heuristic**: break MRV ties by choosing the variable involved in the most constraints on unassigned variables, maximizing the propagation you will get from the assignment.

**Least constraining value**: having chosen a variable, try the value that eliminates the fewest options for its neighbors, maximizing the chance that this branch succeeds.

Note the deliberate asymmetry: choose variables to fail fast, choose values to succeed fast. This is not inconsistent. Variable choice determines the shape of the entire subtree and you want doomed subtrees pruned early; value choice determines which branch you explore first and you want the successful branch first. The asymmetry is a small, elegant piece of algorithmic reasoning worth carrying around.

**Backjumping** improves on chronological backtracking by returning not to the immediately previous decision but to the most recent decision actually implicated in the conflict. **Conflict-driven clause learning**, the technique that made modern SAT solvers practical, goes further: on hitting a conflict, it analyzes the implication graph, derives a new clause capturing the reason for failure, and adds it permanently. The solver thereby *learns*, ensuring it never revisits the same failure by a different route. This is a general and profound idea — converting a failure into an explicit, reusable constraint — and it applies to human problem solving just as well. When a promising approach fails, extracting the *reason* it failed prevents the whole family of similar approaches from being retried.

### Optimization and soft constraints

A CSP asks for any satisfying assignment; a **constrained optimization problem** asks for the best one under an objective function. Linear programming, integer programming, and mixed-integer programming address these where constraints and objective are linear, and the simplex and branch-and-bound methods are the workhorses.

Real problems often have **soft constraints** — preferences that incur a penalty when violated rather than an outright prohibition. Modeling something as hard when it is really soft is a very common specification error, and it produces the frustrating outcome of an infeasible problem where a slightly imperfect solution was available and acceptable. Before declaring "there is no solution," it is always worth asking which constraints are genuinely inviolable. In practice, the answer is usually "fewer than were written down."

## VIII. Sequence and Pattern Reasoning: Induction and Its Discontents

### The fundamental underdetermination

"2, 4, 6, 8, …" — what comes next? The expected answer is 10. But `f(n) = 2n` and `f(n) = 2n + (n-1)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)` agree on the first four terms and diverge wildly afterward; the second gives 34. In fact, for *any* finite sequence and *any* proposed next value, a polynomial exists that fits the given terms and produces that value. Finite data never uniquely determines an infinite rule.

This is not pedantry. It is the core epistemological fact about pattern reasoning, and it explains why "find the next number" puzzles are, strictly speaking, ill-posed. What they actually test is a shared sense of *simplicity* — which rule a reasonable person would consider the most economical fit. That shared sense is real, useful, and formalizable up to a point (via Kolmogorov complexity or the minimum description length principle), but it is a convention layered on top of the logic, not a consequence of it.

Every empirical science lives inside this problem. Data underdetermines theory; simplicity is the tiebreaker; and the tiebreaker occasionally picks wrong. Holding a pattern as *the best current hypothesis* rather than *the answer* is the correct epistemic posture, and it costs nothing.

### Standard families

Recognizing common patterns is a matter of trained pattern-matching against a modest catalog.

**Arithmetic sequences** have constant first differences: `aₙ = a₁ + (n-1)d`. The sum of the first `n` terms is `n(a₁ + aₙ)/2` — the average of first and last, times the count, which is the story behind the Gauss anecdote about summing 1 to 100 by pairing.

**Geometric sequences** have a constant ratio: `aₙ = a₁ r^(n-1)`. Their sum is `a₁(1 - rⁿ)/(1 - r)`, converging to `a₁/(1-r)` when `|r| < 1`. The distinction between arithmetic and geometric growth is the single most consequential quantitative intuition a person can have, and human intuition is systematically biased toward the arithmetic — which is why compound interest, epidemic spread, and Moore's-law scaling all feel surprising long after they have been explained.

**Polynomial sequences** reveal themselves through the method of finite differences. Take successive differences repeatedly; if the `k`-th differences become constant, the sequence is generated by a degree-`k` polynomial, and the leading coefficient is that constant divided by `k!`. For `1, 4, 9, 16, 25` the first differences are `3, 5, 7, 9` and the second are constant at `2`, giving degree 2 with leading coefficient `2/2! = 1` — the squares. This method is fully mechanical and settles a large fraction of sequence puzzles without any inspiration required.

**Recursive sequences** define each term from predecessors. Fibonacci, `F(n) = F(n-1) + F(n-2)`, is the famous case; its ratio of consecutive terms converges to the golden ratio `φ = (1+√5)/2`, and it has a closed form (Binet's formula) despite its recursive definition. The general lesson is that recursive and closed forms are two descriptions of one object, each better for different purposes: recursive for computation and proof, closed for asymptotics and direct evaluation.

**Figurate and combinatorial sequences** — triangular numbers `n(n+1)/2`, factorials, powers of two, Catalan numbers, rows of Pascal's triangle — appear constantly and are worth recognizing on sight.

**Interleaved sequences** hide two patterns in alternating positions: `1, 10, 2, 20, 3, 30` is two sequences shuffled. When differences look chaotic, checking the odd- and even-indexed subsequences separately is a cheap and often decisive move.

**Operational sequences** apply a transformation rather than an arithmetic rule: digit reversals, digit sums, look-and-say (`1, 11, 21, 1211, 111221`, where each term describes the previous one aloud). These resist numerical analysis entirely and are found only by considering that the rule may be about *representation* rather than *value* — a useful reminder that when one class of hypothesis is exhausted, the productive move is to change the class rather than to search harder within it.

### A systematic procedure

When facing an unfamiliar sequence, work through a checklist rather than waiting for insight:

1. Compute first, second, and third differences. Constant differences settle it.
2. Compute ratios. Constant ratios settle it.
3. Check for a mixture: `aₙ = r·aₙ₋₁ + d`.
4. Look for recognizable landmarks — primes, squares, factorials, powers.
5. Split odd- and even-indexed terms.
6. Check whether each term is built from *two or more* prior terms.
7. Consider digits rather than values.
8. Consider a non-numeric source: positions in the alphabet, letter counts of spelled-out numbers, calendar or clock structure.

The checklist matters more than any individual technique, because it converts an open-ended search into a bounded one and prevents the common failure of fixating on the first hypothesis class.

### Induction, formal and informal

There are two things called induction and they are nearly opposites.

**Mathematical induction** is deductive and certain. To prove `P(n)` for all naturals: prove `P(1)`, then prove `P(k) → P(k+1)`. The conclusion follows with full deductive force. **Strong induction** assumes `P(1) … P(k)` to prove `P(k+1)` and is equivalent in power. **Structural induction** generalizes the pattern to trees, lists, and inductively defined data types, and is the standard tool for proving properties of recursive programs.

**Empirical induction** is the inference from observed cases to a general rule. It is not deductive, confers no certainty, and is the entire basis of natural science. Hume's problem — that any justification of induction seems to presuppose induction — has never been dissolved to universal satisfaction, and yet induction works, which is one of the more comfortable unresolved situations in philosophy.

The practical corollary: a conjecture verified for the first ten thousand cases is not proved. Pólya's example is the polynomial `n² + n + 41`, which yields primes for every `n` from 0 to 39 and fails at 40. Skewes' number and the Mertens conjecture provide cases where the first counterexample is astronomically far out. Verification builds confidence; only proof ends the question.

## IX. Quantitative Word Problems: Translation as the Bottleneck

### Where the difficulty actually lives

The arithmetic in a word problem is almost never the hard part. The hard part is **translation**: converting prose into a formal representation whose structure corresponds to the situation described. A person who sets up the right equation and makes a small arithmetic slip has reasoned well and computed poorly, which is a minor and easily-caught failure. A person who computes flawlessly from the wrong equation has failed at the thing that matters and will produce a confident, precise, wrong answer.

Nearly all instruction in this area therefore ought to focus on setup, and nearly all of it focuses on computation instead.

### The translation procedure

**Name the unknowns explicitly, with units.** Not "let x be the speed" but "let `v` = the boat's speed in still water, in km/h." The unit is part of the definition, and ambiguity about which of several similar quantities a symbol denotes is the leading cause of setup errors.

**Convert relational phrases mechanically.** "Three more than twice a number" is `2n + 3`, not `2(n+3)` and not `3n + 2`. "Five less than" is `x - 5`, with the operands in that order and not the other — this reversal alone accounts for a startling share of errors. "The ratio of A to B is 3:5" becomes `A/B = 3/5` or, more usefully, `A = 3k, B = 5k`, which introduces a scaling variable and keeps everything integral.

**Write one equation per independent piece of information.** Count them. `n` unknowns generally require `n` independent equations; if you have fewer, you have missed a constraint in the prose or the problem is underdetermined, and knowing which is the case is essential.

**Check dimensional consistency before solving.** Dimensional analysis is a free and enormously powerful error check: any equation adding a length to a time is wrong, no algebra required. Carrying units through every step catches errors early, and often the units themselves suggest the operation — needing an answer in km/h from a distance in km and a time in h tells you unambiguously to divide.

### The standard problem families

**Rate problems.** Distance = rate × time is the template, and nearly every variant is about being careful with which quantity is shared. Two objects approaching each other have relative speed equal to the sum; moving apart in the same direction, the difference. Boats in currents and planes in wind add or subtract the medium's speed.

The classic trap is averaging rates. A car travels one leg at 60 km/h and returns at 40 km/h; the average speed is not 50. Speed is a ratio, and averaging must be weighted by *time*, not distance — while equal distances mean unequal times. The correct value is the harmonic mean, `2(60)(40)/(60+40) = 48` km/h. The lesson generalizes far past traffic: whenever you average ratios, ask what the denominators are and whether they are equal. Averaging percentages across groups of different sizes is the same error wearing different clothes, and it is committed constantly in reporting on test scores, conversion rates, and returns.

**Work problems.** If A completes a job in `a` hours, A's rate is `1/a` jobs per hour. Rates add for cooperative work: `1/a + 1/b = 1/t`. The conceptual move — from time-per-job to job-per-time — is the whole difficulty, because times do not add and rates do. This same reciprocal move underlies parallel resistors, effective spring constants in series, and lens combinations; recognizing the shared structure is a genuine transfer of understanding rather than a memorized formula.

**Mixture and concentration problems.** Track the *amount of the substance*, not the concentration. Concentrations do not add; quantities do. `0.30(x) + 0.60(y) = 0.45(x+y)` is the correct form, and the error of writing `0.30 + 0.60 = 0.90` is not so much an arithmetic mistake as a failure to identify what is conserved. The habit of asking "what quantity is conserved here?" is one of the most transferable in all of quantitative reasoning.

**Percentage problems.** Percentages require constant vigilance about the base. A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the original: `1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96`, a 4% net loss, because the second percentage applies to a larger base. Percentage points differ from percent: a rise from 5% to 6% is one percentage point and a twenty percent relative increase, and headlines routinely exploit the ambiguity. To reverse a percentage change, divide rather than subtract.

**Age, coin, and digit problems.** These are exercises in careful symbolization. Ages advance in lockstep, so if the difference is `d` now it is `d` forever — a fact that collapses many such problems immediately. A two-digit number with tens digit `t` and units digit `u` has value `10t + u`, not `tu`, and reversing it gives `10u + t`; the difference of a number and its reversal is always a multiple of nine, which is often the key.

### Estimation and Fermi reasoning

A distinct and underrated skill is producing an order-of-magnitude answer with almost no data. Enrico Fermi's canonical exercise — how many piano tuners are in Chicago — is solved by decomposing an unknowable quantity into a chain of guessable ones: population, people per household, fraction with pianos, tuning frequency, tunings per tuner per year. Each factor may be wrong by a factor of two; the errors are partly independent and partly cancel, so the product is typically within an order of magnitude.

The value is twofold. First, many practical decisions need only order-of-magnitude accuracy, and the estimate arrives in two minutes rather than two weeks. Second, and more important, the estimate is a **sanity check** on precise calculations. When a detailed model outputs a number three orders of magnitude away from a back-of-envelope figure, one of them is wrong, and the discrepancy is a gift — it points directly at a modeling error that would otherwise ship. Engineers who habitually estimate before computing catch mistakes that no amount of careful arithmetic would reveal, because careful arithmetic on a wrong model is still wrong.

Related is the discipline of **checking the answer against the question**. Does the sign make sense? Is the magnitude plausible? Did the problem ask for the value of `x` or for the quantity `2x + 5` that `x` was introduced to find? Answering the wrong question correctly is depressingly common and entirely preventable by re-reading the prompt after solving.

## X. Probability Reasoning: Inference Under Uncertainty

### The axioms and what they buy

Kolmogorov's axioms are minimal. For a sample space `Ω` and events as subsets:

1. `P(A) ≥ 0` for every event `A`.
2. `P(Ω) = 1`.
3. For mutually exclusive events, `P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)`.

Everything else follows. `P(¬A) = 1 - P(A)`. `P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)`, the inclusion–exclusion correction that prevents double-counting the overlap. `P(∅) = 0`.

The formalism is trivial; the interpretation is contested. The **frequentist** reading treats probability as the limiting relative frequency of an event in repeated trials, which is clean for dice and awkward for one-off events like "the probability this bridge design fails." The **Bayesian** reading treats probability as a degree of belief, coherently updated, which handles one-off events naturally at the price of admitting subjective priors. Both use identical mathematics. For reasoning purposes the useful stance is pragmatic: use whichever interpretation makes the problem tractable, and be explicit about which you are using, since arguments between the camps are usually arguments about which questions are worth asking.

### Conditional probability and independence

`P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)`, defined when `P(B) > 0`. This is the single most important formula in applied reasoning. It says: restrict attention to the worlds where `B` holds, and ask what fraction of those also have `A`. Conditioning is *renormalization* — throwing away part of the sample space and rescaling what remains.

Events are **independent** when `P(A | B) = P(A)`, equivalently `P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)`. Independence is a strong assumption and it is assumed far more often than it is checked. In reliability engineering, assuming independent component failures gives spectacularly optimistic system reliability figures, because real failures share causes: a common power supply, a common thermal environment, a common manufacturing lot, a common design error. Correlated failure is why redundancy delivers less than the arithmetic promises, and why the same wrong reasoning shows up in financial models of "independent" mortgage defaults.

Note also that pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence, and that independence is not the same as being mutually exclusive — in fact mutually exclusive events with nonzero probability are maximally *dependent*, since knowing one occurred tells you the other did not.

### Bayes' theorem and base rates

`P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)`, with `P(E) = P(E|H)P(H) + P(E|¬H)P(¬H)`.

In words: the posterior probability of a hypothesis given evidence equals the likelihood of the evidence under the hypothesis, times the prior, normalized. It is the formal statement of how belief should change when data arrives, and it is derivable in one line from the definition of conditional probability — a triviality mathematically and a revolution practically.

The canonical demonstration is medical testing. Suppose a condition affects 1 in 1000 people. A test has 99% sensitivity (detects the condition when present) and 95% specificity (correctly negative when absent). Someone tests positive. What is the probability they have the condition?

Most people, including a majority of clinicians in published studies, answer around 95–99%. The correct answer is about 2%.

The arithmetic is easiest in **natural frequencies**. Imagine 100,000 people. About 100 have the condition; the test catches 99 of them. About 99,900 do not; with 95% specificity, 5% of them — 4,995 people — test positive anyway. Total positives: `99 + 4,995 = 5,094`. Of these, 99 actually have the condition. `99 / 5,094 ≈ 1.9%`.

The false positives swamp the true positives because the base rate is low, and this is not a quirk of the numbers but a structural fact about screening rare conditions. **Base rate neglect** — reasoning from `P(E|H)` while ignoring `P(H)` — is among the most consequential reasoning errors in existence, appearing in medical diagnosis, security screening, fraud detection, anomaly alerts in monitoring systems, and criminal forensics.

Two practical antidotes. First, reframe in natural frequencies rather than percentages; the error rate in experiments drops dramatically with no other intervention, because counts of people are cognitively tractable in a way that conditional percentages are not. Second, ask the diagnostic question explicitly: *how common is this to begin with?* A test result multiplies your prior odds by the likelihood ratio; it does not replace them.

The odds form of Bayes makes this vivid: `posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio`. With a likelihood ratio of about 20 (0.99/0.05) and prior odds of 1:999, posterior odds are about 20:999 — roughly 2%. A strong test is a multiplier, and a multiplier applied to a tiny number stays small.

### The prosecutor's fallacy and inverted conditionals

`P(A|B)` and `P(B|A)` are different quantities and confusing them is the deepest recurring error in probabilistic reasoning. `P(positive | disease)` is 0.99; `P(disease | positive)` is 0.02. Both statements are true about the same test.

In court, the **prosecutor's fallacy** presents `P(evidence | innocent)` — say, a one-in-a-million DNA match probability — as if it were `P(innocent | evidence)`. In a city of ten million, a one-in-a-million random match rate implies roughly ten unrelated people would match. The evidence is strong but not conclusive, and the difference between "one in a million" and "one of about ten" has decided real cases wrongly.

The same inversion appears in engineering as "this alarm only fires falsely 1% of the time, so a firing means a 99% chance of a real fault" — false whenever real faults are rarer than 1% of observation windows, which is usually.

### Combinatorics as the counting substrate

Discrete probability rests on counting. **Permutations** count ordered arrangements: `P(n,k) = n!/(n-k)!`. **Combinations** count unordered selections: `C(n,k) = n!/(k!(n-k)!)`. The distinction — does order matter? — should be settled explicitly before any counting begins, because the two answers differ by a factor of `k!` and the resulting error is silent.

Two counting principles solve most problems. The **multiplication principle**: independent sequential choices multiply. The **complement principle**: when "at least one" appears, compute `1 - P(none)`, since "none" is a single case and "at least one" is many overlapping ones.

The **birthday problem** demonstrates both. With 23 people, the probability that two share a birthday exceeds 1/2. Direct computation over shared pairs is a mess; the complement is easy. `P(all distinct) = (365/365)(364/365)…(343/365) ≈ 0.493`, so `P(shared) ≈ 0.507`. The intuition fails because people count *people* (23) rather than *pairs* (253), and the number of opportunities for a coincidence grows quadratically. Whenever a coincidence seems surprising, the first question should be how many chances it had to occur — a question that dissolves most apparent statistical miracles, including the near-certainty that *some* extremely unlikely thing will happen to *someone* today.

### Expectation, variance, and their limits

The **expected value** `E[X] = Σ xᵢ P(xᵢ)` is the probability-weighted average. Its linearity — `E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]` regardless of dependence — is a remarkably strong tool that lets otherwise brutal problems dissolve.

But expectation is not always the right decision criterion. The **St. Petersburg paradox** offers a game with infinite expected value that nobody would pay much to play, because outcomes are dominated by vanishingly rare enormous payoffs. Utility is typically concave in wealth, so the expected *utility* is finite and modest. More practically: a gamble with positive expectation but a meaningful chance of ruin is a bad gamble, because ruin is absorbing and removes you from all future gambles. The distinction between **ensemble averages** (across many parallel players) and **time averages** (one player across many rounds) matters enormously whenever losses compound, and confusing them is the mathematical root of a great deal of financial disaster.

**Variance** quantifies spread, `Var(X) = E[(X - μ)²]`, and unlike expectation it is not linear: `Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)` requires independence. This asymmetry is the reason diversification works and the reason correlated risks are so dangerous.

The **law of large numbers** says the sample mean converges to the true mean as `n` grows. It does *not* say that deviations get corrected. The **gambler's fallacy** — believing a run of reds makes black "due" — misreads convergence as compensation. Independent trials have no memory; the mean converges because early deviations are diluted by later data, not because they are repaid. The mirror-image error, the **hot hand fallacy** in its naive form, assumes streaks predict continuation. Both mistakes stem from a refusal to accept that random sequences look far clumpier than intuition expects; genuinely random data contains runs, and their absence is itself evidence of non-randomness.

### Monty Hall and the value of conditioning on the mechanism

Three doors; a prize behind one. You pick door 1. The host, who knows the layout, opens door 3 to reveal a goat and offers a switch. Should you?

Yes — switching wins with probability 2/3. The initial pick is right 1/3 of the time; the host's opening does not change that, since he could always open a goat door regardless. The remaining 2/3 concentrates entirely on the unopened door.

The crucial and usually-omitted point is that the answer depends on the *host's protocol*. If the host opened a door at random and it happened to show a goat, the probability is 1/2 either way. Same observation, different mechanism, different inference. This is the deepest lesson in the puzzle and the reason it belongs in a discussion of reasoning rather than a discussion of arithmetic: **the informational content of an observation depends on the process that generated it**, and conditioning on the observation while ignoring the selection process yields wrong answers. Survivorship bias, publication bias, and selection effects in data collection are all instances of exactly this failure.

### Distributions worth recognizing

A short catalog covers most modeling needs. The **binomial** counts successes in `n` independent trials with fixed probability `p`, with mean `np`. The **Poisson** models rare events over a continuous interval, with mean equal to variance — arrivals, defects, decay counts. The **geometric** counts trials until the first success and is memoryless. The **normal** arises wherever many small independent effects add, by the central limit theorem, which is why it appears everywhere and also why it fails where effects multiply rather than add — in which case the **lognormal** applies, with its long right tail. **Power-law** distributions, common in network and financial phenomena, may have infinite variance, so sample means are unstable and standard confidence intervals mislead badly. Applying normal-distribution intuitions to power-law data is a well-documented route to underestimating extreme risk.

## XI. Algorithmic Reasoning: Constructing Reliable Procedures

### What an algorithm is

An algorithm is a finite, unambiguous procedure that transforms input to output and terminates. Each clause carries weight: *finite* excludes infinite descriptions, *unambiguous* excludes steps requiring judgment, *terminates* excludes procedures that may run forever.

Algorithmic reasoning differs from the reasoning discussed so far in a fundamental way. Deduction, syllogism, and probability evaluate given claims. Algorithmics *constructs* something and then must argue that the construction works — for all inputs, not merely the ones tried. It is closer to engineering than to analysis, and it demands the two questions that define the field: **is it correct?** and **what does it cost?**

### Correctness: invariants and termination

A **loop invariant** is a property true before the loop begins, preserved by each iteration, and strong enough at exit to establish what you want. Proving one requires three steps — initialization, maintenance, termination — and is structurally identical to mathematical induction, with the loop counter as the induction variable.

For binary search, the invariant is: *if the target is present, it lies within `[lo, hi]`.* Initialization is trivial since the range is the whole array. Maintenance holds because comparing against the midpoint eliminates only a half that provably cannot contain the target. At termination the range is empty or a singleton, so the answer is determined. The proof is three sentences and it covers infinitely many inputs — which is the point. Testing binary search on a thousand arrays establishes far less than this paragraph does.

**Termination** requires a **variant**: a quantity that strictly decreases with each iteration and is bounded below. For binary search, `hi - lo`. Termination arguments are where the classic binary search bug lives: an implementation that computes `mid` and then sets `lo = mid` rather than `mid + 1` can leave the range unchanged, so the variant does not decrease and the loop hangs on a two-element range. Requiring an explicit variant catches this before it ships. Knuth's remark that binary search was first published in 1946 and the first correct implementation of the general case in 1962 is a permanent reminder that "obviously correct" and "correct" are different predicates.

### Complexity: the cost of a procedure

**Big-O** notation describes asymptotic upper bounds: `f(n) = O(g(n))` if `f` grows no faster than `g` up to a constant. `Ω` gives lower bounds and `Θ` gives tight ones.

The practical hierarchy: `O(1)` constant, `O(log n)` logarithmic, `O(n)` linear, `O(n log n)` linearithmic, `O(n²)` quadratic, `O(2ⁿ)` exponential, `O(n!)` factorial. The gaps are not gentle. At `n = 1,000,000`, a logarithmic algorithm does about twenty operations and a quadratic one does a trillion. No hardware improvement bridges that; a thousandfold faster machine running a quadratic algorithm is beaten by a linearithmic one on modestly larger inputs, and the crossover point moves *toward* the better algorithm as `n` grows.

Three caveats keep asymptotic reasoning honest. Constants matter at realistic sizes — an `O(n log n)` algorithm with enormous overhead loses to `O(n²)` for small `n`, which is why production sort implementations switch to insertion sort below a threshold. Worst case is not average case; quicksort is `O(n²)` in the worst case and the fastest general-purpose sort in practice. And memory hierarchy effects can dominate operation counts entirely, since a cache-friendly quadratic scan may beat a pointer-chasing linearithmic structure.

### Design paradigms

**Divide and conquer** splits a problem into independent subproblems, solves recursively, and combines. Merge sort, quicksort, fast Fourier transform, Karatsuba multiplication. The Master Theorem gives the resulting complexity for recurrences of the form `T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n)`, and the intuition it encodes is worth internalizing: the answer depends on whether the work is dominated by the leaves, the root, or spread evenly across levels.

**Dynamic programming** applies when subproblems *overlap*. Naive recursive Fibonacci is exponential because it recomputes the same values astronomically often; memoizing makes it linear. The requirements are optimal substructure (an optimal solution contains optimal solutions to subproblems) and overlapping subproblems. Shortest paths, edit distance, knapsack, sequence alignment. DP is essentially the systematic application of the principle *never compute the same thing twice*, and recognizing when it applies is largely a matter of noticing recomputation in a recursion tree.

**Greedy algorithms** make the locally optimal choice at each step and never reconsider. They are fast and simple, and they are correct only when the problem has a specific structure (formally, a matroid, or an exchange-argument-friendly ordering). Dijkstra's algorithm, Huffman coding, and activity selection are greedy and correct. Greedy fails on 0/1 knapsack, and it fails on Dijkstra's own problem when edges may be negative. The instructive point is that greedy algorithms *always produce an answer* — they simply produce a wrong one when the structure is absent, silently and confidently. A greedy approach must be proved, not merely observed to work on examples.

**Backtracking and branch-and-bound** perform systematic search with pruning, as discussed under constraint problems. **Randomized algorithms** trade determinism for speed or simplicity, either always-correct-but-variable-time (Las Vegas) or fast-but-occasionally-wrong-with-bounded-probability (Monte Carlo). **Approximation algorithms** accept a provable ratio to optimal in exchange for tractability, which is the honest response to NP-hardness: if you cannot have optimal, have a guarantee.

### Decomposition and abstraction

The most transferable habit in algorithmic reasoning is **decomposition**: breaking a problem into parts with clearly specified interfaces, so each can be reasoned about independently. The interface is the contract — preconditions, postconditions, invariants — and once it is fixed, a component's internals become irrelevant to the rest of the argument. This is the only known way to reason about systems larger than a person can hold in mind at once, and it is why modularity is not a stylistic preference but an epistemic necessity.

**Abstraction** is the complementary move: identifying that two apparently different problems share a structure, so a solution transfers. Recognizing that scheduling, register allocation, and frequency assignment are all graph coloring means one algorithm serves three domains. Recognizing that a physical filter and a moving average are the same operation in different notations means an intuition built in one transfers to the other. Most of what makes an experienced problem-solver fast is not superior processing but a larger stock of recognized structures.

### What algorithms cannot do

Some problems admit no algorithm at all. The **halting problem** — deciding whether an arbitrary program halts on an arbitrary input — is undecidable, proved by Turing in 1936 via a diagonalization argument: assume a decider exists, construct a program that consults it about itself and does the opposite, derive contradiction. Rice's theorem generalizes: every non-trivial semantic property of programs is undecidable.

This is not an engineering limitation to be overcome by better tools. It is a mathematical boundary, and it explains why static analyzers must be either incomplete (missing real bugs) or unsound (reporting false ones), why perfect virus detection is impossible in principle, and why compiler optimization is inherently conservative. Knowing that a boundary is logical rather than technological saves an enormous amount of wasted effort, and recognizing when a request implicitly demands the impossible is itself a valuable form of reasoning.

## XII. Step-by-Step Problem Solving: The Discipline That Binds the Rest

### Pólya's four phases

George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) remains the most useful short account of problem-solving method, and its four phases are worth stating because people reliably skip the first and the last.

**1. Understand the problem.** What is the unknown? What is given? What is the condition connecting them? Can you restate it in your own words? Can you draw it? Is the condition sufficient to determine the unknown — or insufficient, or redundant, or contradictory? Most failures happen here, invisibly, because solving feels productive and understanding feels like delay.

**2. Devise a plan.** Have you seen a related problem? Is there a problem with the same unknown? Can you solve a special case, a simpler version, a part of the problem? Can you work backwards from the goal? Can you vary the problem — generalize it, specialize it, drop a condition — until it becomes tractable?

**3. Carry out the plan.** Execute each step, checking it. Can you see clearly that each step is correct? Can you prove it? This phase demands care rather than creativity, and it is the only phase most instruction addresses.

**4. Look back.** Check the result. Check the argument. Can you derive the result differently? Can you use the result or the method for another problem? This is the phase that converts a solved problem into transferable knowledge, and it is nearly always skipped, which is why people solve many problems and learn from few.

### The heuristic toolkit

Beyond the phases, a set of concrete moves recurs across every domain in this essay.

**Solve a simpler version.** Reduce the size, the dimension, the number of cases. A problem about `n` items often yields to examining `n = 1, 2, 3` until the structure appears. This is how conjectures are born, and it costs almost nothing.

**Work backwards.** Start from the goal and ask what would immediately produce it. Maze-solving, proof construction, and planning problems are frequently far easier in reverse, because the goal is more constrained than the start.

**Look for an invariant.** Ask what does not change. A quantity, a parity, a colouring, a conserved sum. Invariants are the most powerful single tool for impossibility proofs: if an operation preserves a property and the target lacks it, the target is unreachable. The classic mutilated-chessboard argument — a board missing two opposite corners cannot be tiled by dominoes, because each domino covers one square of each colour and the removed corners share a colour — is a two-line proof of something that resists exhaustive search entirely.

**Consider extreme and degenerate cases.** The largest element, the smallest, the empty case, the boundary. Extremes often force structure, and degenerate cases are where both proofs and programs break.

**Use symmetry.** If the problem is symmetric in two variables, the solution likely is too, which halves the search. If a proposed solution breaks a symmetry the problem possesses, that is a signal something is wrong.

**Change the representation.** This is the most powerful move available and the hardest to invoke on demand. Karnaugh maps turn algebra into geometry. Venn diagrams turn syllogisms into pictures. Natural frequencies turn Bayesian arithmetic into counting. Generating functions turn recurrences into algebra. Coordinates turn geometry into equations, and occasionally the reverse move — turning equations into geometry — is what dissolves a problem. When you are stuck, ask not "how do I solve this?" but "in what form would this be easy?"

**Introduce auxiliary elements.** A construction line, a helper variable, an intermediate quantity. Adding structure sometimes reduces difficulty, which is counterintuitive and often decisive.

**Argue by contradiction.** Assume the negation of what you want and derive absurdity. This gives you an extra premise to work with, which is frequently the difference between a hard direct proof and an easy indirect one.

**Count two ways.** Computing the same quantity by two different methods and equating them proves identities that resist direct manipulation. This is the combinatorial version of the general principle that redundant derivations are checks.

### The bookkeeping discipline

Across constraint puzzles, truth tables, algebraic manipulation, and probability trees, the same practical rule appeared: **write everything down**. Working memory holds a handful of items; problems routinely involve dozens. Every deduction held in the head is a deduction at risk, and every re-derivation is a fresh chance to err.

Externalizing state does three things. It frees cognitive capacity for the parts that actually require thought. It creates an audit trail, so an error can be located rather than merely suspected. And it makes the reasoning inspectable by others, which is the precondition for collaboration and review. The habit is unglamorous and its returns are enormous.

A related discipline: **state assumptions explicitly**. Most reasoning failures in practice are not invalid inferences but unnoticed premises — an assumed independence, an assumed non-emptiness, an assumed unit, an assumed range. Writing assumptions down converts them from invisible commitments into checkable claims. When a conclusion turns out to be wrong and the derivation is valid, the assumption list is where the error lives, and having one turns debugging from archaeology into lookup.

### Metacognition: monitoring the process

Skilled problem-solvers differ from novices less in the moves they know than in their monitoring. They notice when an approach is not working and abandon it. They budget effort. They ask whether they are solving the stated problem or a substituted easier one — a substitution that happens automatically and unconsciously, as Kahneman documented, and that produces confident answers to questions nobody asked.

Three checks are worth making habitual:

*Am I answering the question that was asked?* Re-read the prompt after solving. The rate at which correct derivations answer the wrong question is far higher than anyone expects.

*Does this answer pass an independent check?* Substitute back into the original conditions. Estimate the magnitude by another route. Test the algorithm on a boundary case. An answer confirmed by a second, structurally different method is far more trustworthy than one confirmed by re-checking the same derivation, because re-checking reproduces the original error faithfully.

*What would change my mind?* If no evidence could alter a conclusion, the conclusion is not being held for reasons and the reasoning has become decoration. This question is the practical form of falsifiability and it is the strongest available guard against motivated reasoning.

### Knowing when to stop

A less-discussed skill is recognizing when further reasoning has negative expected value. Some problems are underdetermined by available information, and the correct output is "the data does not settle this," stated clearly, rather than a confident answer manufactured to fill the silence. Some problems are provably impossible, and effort spent is wasted. Some decisions are dominated — every option leads to acceptable outcomes — and analysis costs more than it saves.

Calibrating effort to stakes and tractability is itself a reasoning task, and it is the one most often neglected. The reasoner's goal is not maximal rigour everywhere; it is rigour allocated where error would be costly.

## XIII. Synthesis: The Disciplines in Combination

Real problems rarely announce which discipline they belong to, and most substantial ones require several. A worked composite makes the interplay concrete.

**The problem.** A monitoring system watches a fleet of 500 industrial devices. Each device reports a fault flag. Historical data says roughly 1% of devices are genuinely faulty at any time. The detector raises an alarm for 98% of genuinely faulty devices and for 3% of healthy ones. Maintenance capacity allows five devices per day to be inspected. The team wants a rule: given the day's alarms, which devices should be inspected, and is the current detector good enough?

**Deductive and conditional layer.** The specification says "if a device is faulty, an alarm shall be raised." Note immediately that this is a conditional, and that its converse — "if an alarm is raised, the device is faulty" — has not been asserted and does not follow. The whole subsequent analysis exists because the team's intuition silently supplies the converse. Note also that the specification is satisfiable vacuously if no device is ever faulty, so a companion requirement about false alarm rate is needed; the given 3% is that companion.

**Probabilistic layer.** Of 500 devices, about 5 are faulty; the detector catches about 4.9 of them. Of the ~495 healthy devices, 3% — about 14.85 — alarm anyway. Expected alarms per day: roughly 20. Of those, about a quarter are real. `P(faulty | alarm) ≈ 4.9 / 19.75 ≈ 25%`. This is twenty-five times the base rate, so the detector is genuinely informative; it is also nowhere near the certainty the team assumed.

**Quantitative layer.** Twenty alarms against five inspection slots is a resource conflict, and the numbers show it is structural rather than incidental. Options: raise the alarm threshold to reduce false positives (at the cost of missing faults), add a second independent check to multiply the likelihood ratio, or expand capacity. Each is a quantifiable trade-off, and each should be evaluated with an explicit cost of a missed fault versus a wasted inspection. Without those two numbers, the question "is the detector good enough?" has no answer — a point worth stating plainly rather than papering over.

**The independence caveat.** A second detector helps only insofar as its errors are uncorrelated with the first's. If both read the same sensor, they share a failure mode and the combined likelihood ratio is far below the product of the individual ones. This is exactly the independence assumption discussed earlier, and it must be checked empirically rather than assumed.

**Constraint layer.** Which five to inspect becomes a CSP with an objective: maximize expected faults found, subject to capacity, technician skill matching, geographic routing, and any device that has alarmed three days running. Written down formally, it is a small optimization problem with a standard solution method — and writing it down formally is what reveals that "technician skill matching" was a soft constraint being treated as hard, which was making some days' schedules infeasible for no good reason.

**Pattern layer.** Devices alarming repeatedly deserve attention not because each alarm is strong but because repeated independent alarms multiply the likelihood ratio. Three alarms in three days, if genuinely independent, take posterior odds from about 1:3 to something overwhelming. Here the sequence carries far more information than any single reading — but only if the alarms are independent, which for a device with a slowly degrading sensor they are not.

**Algorithmic layer.** The daily selection must run automatically. It needs a correctness argument (the selected set always satisfies capacity and skill constraints), a termination argument, and a complexity budget — trivial at 500 devices, worth checking at 500,000.

**Step-by-step layer.** Throughout, the assumptions are written down: base rate 1%, sensitivity 98%, specificity 3% false positive, independence across devices and across days. Each is a checkable empirical claim, and each is the kind of thing that quietly stops being true. The analysis is only as good as its weakest assumption, and the value of listing them is that when the system misbehaves in six months, the list is where to look first.

The point of the example is not the arithmetic. It is that a single mundane operational question drew on conditional logic, Bayesian inference, combinatorial estimation, constraint optimization, sequence reasoning, and algorithmic correctness — and that the most consequential single move in the whole analysis was noticing, in the first paragraph, that a conditional had been silently converted.

## XIV. Closing: What Transfers

If this essay has a single thesis, it is that the disciplines surveyed here are not a list of techniques but a set of overlapping lenses, and that fluency consists in knowing which lens to pick up.

A handful of principles recur in every section, and they are what actually transfers:

**Form and content are separable, and separating them is the whole trick.** Validity is structural. The same schema governs mortality and null pointers. Learning to see the skeleton of an argument through its subject matter is the foundational skill.

**Negation is where reasoning breaks.** De Morgan's laws, contraposition, the complement rule in probability, the difference between "not always" and "always not." Handle negation mechanically rather than intuitively, every time.

**Conditioning is not commutative.** `P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A)`. Sufficient is not necessary. A statement is not its converse. This one distinction, in its several disguises, accounts for a large fraction of all reasoning error.

**The generating process determines what an observation means.** Monty Hall, selection bias, survivorship, vacuous satisfaction, don't-care conditions. Ask not only what you observed but how the observation came to reach you.

**Externalize state.** Truth table columns, puzzle grids, assumption lists, invariants. Paper does not forget and does not get tired.

**Simplicity is a tiebreaker, not a proof.** Finite data underdetermines the rule. Verified cases are not a theorem. Hold hypotheses with the modality they deserve.

**Check by a second route.** Dimensional analysis against algebra. Estimate against computation. Diagram against symbols. An independent confirmation is worth ten re-readings of the same derivation.

**Know the shape of the boundary.** Some problems are exponential, some are undecidable, some are underdetermined by the data. Recognizing which wall you are facing prevents effort from being spent on the impossible and directs it toward the tractable reformulation that usually exists nearby.

None of this makes reasoning automatic. The errors catalogued here are not exotic; they are the default outputs of a cognitive system optimized for speed and social coordination rather than for formal correctness, and they recur in people who know better, including people who have just finished writing about them. What the disciplines offer is not immunity but a set of checkpoints — places where a habit of asking one specific question intercepts an error that would otherwise pass unnoticed.

That is a modest claim, and it is the honest one. The value of studying reasoning formally is not that it makes you right. It is that it gives you a vocabulary for being wrong in ways you can find.

## Appendix: A Practice Set

Problems that exercise the material, ordered roughly by section. Solutions are deliberately omitted; the *look back* phase is where the learning is.

**Deduction.** Identify the form and assess validity: "If the firmware is corrupted, the checksum fails. The checksum passed. Therefore the firmware is not corrupted." Then the same with the last two lines swapped.

**Conditional.** Write the converse, inverse, and contrapositive of: "A device enters safe mode only if the temperature exceeds 85°C." Note carefully which of "if" and "only if" you were given.

**Boolean.** Simplify `(A ∧ B) ∨ (A ∧ ¬B ∧ C) ∨ (¬A ∧ B)` to three literals or fewer. Then express it using only NAND.

**Truth tables.** Determine by the shortcut method whether `(P ∨ Q) → R` entails `(P → R) ∧ (Q → R)`, and whether the converse entailment holds.

**Syllogism.** Assess: "No compilers are interpreters. Some tools are interpreters. Therefore some tools are not compilers." Identify the mood, the figure, and the distribution of each term.

**Constraints.** Four engineers — one each in firmware, PCB, RF, and test — sit in a row. The RF engineer is not at either end. The firmware engineer sits immediately left of the test engineer. The PCB engineer is somewhere left of the RF engineer. Determine the order.

**Sequence.** Continue and justify: `2, 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, …` Then: `1, 11, 21, 1211, 111221, …` Note which technique each required and why the first failed on the second.

**Quantitative.** A tank is filled by pipe A in 6 hours and drained by pipe B in 10. Both open, starting empty: how long to fill? Now suppose B only opens once the tank is half full.

**Probability.** A factory has three lines producing 50%, 30%, and 20% of output, with defect rates 1%, 2%, and 4%. A defective unit is found. What is the probability it came from line 3? Compare your answer to the prior of 20% and explain the direction of the shift.

**Algorithmic.** Write a loop invariant for an in-place array reversal and prove maintenance. Then state the variant that establishes termination and identify the off-by-one error that the variant catches.

**Composite.** Take any recurring decision you actually make — a purchase, a schedule, a diagnostic procedure — and write it out as: the conditionals assumed, the constraints binding, the quantities estimated, the probabilities involved, and the procedure followed. Then find the one assumption in your list that you have never checked.
