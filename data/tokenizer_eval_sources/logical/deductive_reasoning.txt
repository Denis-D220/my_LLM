DEDUCTIVE REASONING: FORM, VALIDITY, AND PROOF

Deductive reasoning is the drawing of conclusions that follow necessarily from stated premises. Its defining property is that if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. This is a far stronger guarantee than any other form of inference provides. Inductive reasoning generalizes from cases and can be overturned by a new case. Abductive reasoning proposes the best available explanation and can be displaced by a better one. Deduction alone offers necessity, and that necessity comes from form rather than from content.

The price of this guarantee is that deduction never produces information not already contained in the premises. It rearranges and makes explicit what was implicitly present. This is not a weakness. Most of mathematics, most of formal verification, and a large part of careful legal and philosophical argument consists precisely of making explicit what was hidden in a set of assumptions, and the fact that a conclusion was already implied does not make it obvious.

VALIDITY AND SOUNDNESS

An argument is valid when it is impossible for all the premises to be true and the conclusion false simultaneously. Note carefully what this does not require. It does not require that the premises be true. It does not require that the conclusion be true. It does not require that the premises be relevant or interesting. It makes a claim only about the relationship between the premise set and the conclusion.

An argument is sound when it is valid and all of its premises are in fact true. Soundness is what we want in practice; validity is what logic alone can certify. Logic examines the form of inference and is deliberately indifferent to subject matter, and this indifference is exactly what makes it transferable. The same pattern that governs claims about mortality governs claims about voltages, statutes, or database records.

Consider the following:

All planets in this system are made of cheese.
Mars is a planet in this system.
Therefore, Mars is made of cheese.

This argument is perfectly valid and its conclusion is false, because the first premise is false. No logical error occurred; the input was wrong.

Now consider:

Some birds can swim.
A penguin is a bird.
Therefore, a penguin can swim.

Both premises are true and so is the conclusion, yet the argument is invalid. "Some birds can swim" does not license an inference about any particular bird. The conclusion is true for reasons the argument fails to supply. This is the more dangerous failure, because a true conclusion conceals a broken pattern, and the same pattern applied to a different bird would produce a false conclusion.

The practical discipline is to evaluate form and content separately. Ask first whether the conclusion would follow if the premises were granted. Ask second whether the premises should be granted. Confusing these two questions is the root of a great many argumentative failures.

PROPOSITIONAL LOGIC

Propositional logic treats whole statements as atoms and studies how they combine. A proposition is a statement with a definite truth value: true or false, not both. Questions, commands, and expressions of preference are not propositions in this sense.

Propositions are combined with connectives. Negation, written as not P, is true exactly when P is false. Conjunction, P and Q, is true only when both conjuncts are true. Disjunction, P or Q, is true when at least one disjunct is true; this is inclusive disjunction, and it differs from ordinary English usage, where "or" often carries an exclusive sense. Exclusive disjunction, true when exactly one of the two holds, must be written separately when intended.

The conditional, if P then Q, deserves the most attention because it causes the most confusion. In classical logic the conditional is false only when P is true and Q is false. It is true in all other cases, including when P is false. This means "if the moon is made of cheese, then two plus two equals five" is true, which strikes most people as wrong. The apparent oddity dissolves once one recognizes that the material conditional is a truth-functional approximation of a much richer family of everyday conditionals that carry connotations of causation, relevance, and counterfactual support. The material conditional captures only the constraint that you never have a true antecedent with a false consequent. For deductive purposes this is sufficient and is what makes the connective mechanically usable.

In a conditional P implies Q, P is the antecedent and Q the consequent. Three related conditionals must be distinguished. The converse is Q implies P. The inverse is not-P implies not-Q. The contrapositive is not-Q implies not-P. The contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original; the converse and inverse are not. Confusing a conditional with its converse is one of the most common errors in ordinary reasoning. "All engineers know algebra" does not imply "all who know algebra are engineers."

The biconditional, P if and only if Q, is true when P and Q have the same truth value. It asserts both P implies Q and Q implies P.

TRUTH TABLES

A truth table enumerates every possible assignment of truth values to the atomic propositions and computes the resulting value of a compound expression. With n atomic propositions there are 2 to the power n rows.

For two propositions P and Q there are four rows. Conjunction is true in exactly one of them, the row where both are true. Disjunction is true in three. The conditional is true in three, false only in the row where P is true and Q is false. The biconditional is true in two, the rows where P and Q agree.

Truth tables provide a mechanical decision procedure for propositional logic. To test whether an argument is valid, construct a table with columns for all premises and for the conclusion, and inspect every row in which all premises are true. If the conclusion is true in every such row, the argument is valid. If there exists even one row where all premises are true and the conclusion is false, that row is a counterexample and the argument is invalid.

An expression true in every row is a tautology. An expression false in every row is a contradiction. An expression true in some rows and false in others is contingent. Two expressions are logically equivalent when their columns match in every row.

Tautologies include the law of excluded middle, P or not-P, and the law of non-contradiction, not both P and not-P. Both are theorems of classical logic, though certain non-classical systems reject or restrict the first.

The method scales badly. Ten propositions require 1,024 rows and twenty require over a million, which is why derivation systems and more efficient algorithms matter for anything beyond small examples.

VALID ARGUMENT FORMS

A modest number of forms recur constantly and are worth knowing by name.

Modus ponens, the mode that affirms: if P then Q; P; therefore Q. This is the workhorse of deductive inference.

Modus tollens, the mode that denies: if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. If a working circuit implies a lit indicator, and the indicator is dark, the circuit is not working. Modus tollens underlies falsification in empirical testing: a theory predicts an observation, the observation fails, and the theory is rejected, subject to the complication that theories are usually tested together with auxiliary assumptions.

Hypothetical syllogism, chaining conditionals: if P then Q; if Q then R; therefore if P then R. Chains of this kind support extended derivations.

Disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. Eliminating alternatives is legitimate only when the disjunction genuinely exhausts the possibilities.

Constructive dilemma: if P then Q; if R then S; P or R; therefore Q or S. This handles case analysis where one of two situations must hold.

Simplification: P and Q; therefore P. Conjunction: P; Q; therefore P and Q. Addition: P; therefore P or Q. Addition looks strange but is valid, since a disjunction requires only one true disjunct.

Reductio ad absurdum: assume the negation of what is to be proved, derive a contradiction, and conclude the original claim. This is the engine behind many classic proofs, including the irrationality of the square root of two and the infinitude of primes.

De Morgan's laws govern the interaction of negation with conjunction and disjunction. The negation of a conjunction is the disjunction of the negations: not (P and Q) is equivalent to not-P or not-Q. The negation of a disjunction is the conjunction of the negations: not (P or Q) is equivalent to not-P and not-Q. These are constantly needed when negating compound conditions, and getting them wrong is a common source of both logical and programming errors.

FORMAL FALLACIES

A formal fallacy is an invalid pattern, invalid by its structure regardless of content.

Affirming the consequent: if P then Q; Q; therefore P. If it rained, the ground is wet; the ground is wet; therefore it rained. The ground could be wet from a sprinkler. This fallacy is the invalid mirror of modus ponens and is extremely common, because it resembles the valid form closely and because in many everyday cases the conclusion happens to be probable even though it is not entailed. Notice that the inference from evidence to explanation, which is abduction, has exactly this shape; abduction is a legitimate form of reasoning but delivers a plausible hypothesis, not a guaranteed conclusion, and treating an abductive conclusion as deductive is the error.

Denying the antecedent: if P then Q; not P; therefore not Q. If it rained, the ground is wet; it did not rain; therefore the ground is not wet. Again the sprinkler defeats it. This is the invalid mirror of modus tollens.

Illicit conversion treats a conditional as equivalent to its converse. Undistributed middle occurs in categorical arguments when the term shared by both premises is not taken universally in either: all dogs are mammals, all cats are mammals, therefore all dogs are cats.

Recognizing these patterns is a practical skill, and the most reliable technique is to substitute uncontroversial content into the same form. If the form produces an obviously false conclusion from obviously true premises, the form is invalid, and the original argument is invalid too regardless of how plausible its conclusion appeared.

INFORMAL FALLACIES

Informal fallacies are failures of relevance, evidence, or clarity rather than of structure. They are not detectable by inspecting form alone and require judgment about context.

The straw man misrepresents an opponent's position as a weaker one and refutes that instead. The ad hominem attacks the person rather than the argument, though noting a relevant conflict of interest is legitimate when assessing testimony rather than dismissing an argument that stands on its own. Appeal to authority is fallacious when the authority is outside their domain, when experts disagree, or when the claim is offered as proof rather than as evidence; deferring to genuine expertise within its domain is reasonable, not fallacious. False dilemma presents two options as exhaustive when more exist. Slippery slope asserts an inevitable chain of consequences without establishing the links. Circular reasoning assumes the conclusion among the premises. Equivocation shifts the meaning of a term between premises. Composition infers that a whole has a property because its parts do; division reverses the error. Appeal to ignorance treats absence of disproof as proof, though in a context where evidence would be expected if the claim were true, its absence does carry genuine evidential weight.

The correct use of this vocabulary is diagnostic rather than dismissive. Naming a fallacy identifies where an argument needs repair; it does not establish that the conclusion is false, since an argument can be badly made for a true claim.

CATEGORICAL LOGIC

Categorical logic, developed in antiquity and dominant until the nineteenth century, analyzes statements about classes. Four standard forms exist. The universal affirmative asserts that all S are P. The universal negative asserts that no S are P. The particular affirmative asserts that some S are P. The particular negative asserts that some S are not P. In this system "some" means at least one, not "some but not all."

A categorical syllogism has two premises and a conclusion, containing exactly three terms: the major term appearing as the predicate of the conclusion, the minor term as its subject, and the middle term appearing in both premises but not in the conclusion. The middle term is what links the two premises.

Validity depends on the arrangement. Several rules identify invalid arrangements. The middle term must be distributed, meaning taken universally, in at least one premise. Any term distributed in the conclusion must be distributed in the premise where it appears. Two negative premises yield no conclusion. If either premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative. And on the modern reading, which does not assume that categories have members, a particular conclusion cannot be drawn from two universal premises.

Venn diagrams provide an intuitive test. Draw three overlapping circles, one per term. Shade regions the premises declare empty and mark with an x regions the premises declare occupied, always processing universal premises before particular ones so that the placement of an x is not left ambiguous. The argument is valid if the diagram thereby produced already displays what the conclusion asserts, without further additions.

The existential import question is worth noting because it separates the traditional and modern readings. Traditional logic treated "all S are P" as implying that some S exist, which validates inferences the modern reading rejects. Modern logic treats it as a conditional claim about anything that happens to be an S, which is vacuously true if no S exists. Statements about empty categories are the point where the two systems diverge.

PREDICATE LOGIC AND QUANTIFIERS

Propositional logic cannot express internal structure. It cannot represent the relationship between "all humans are mortal" and "Socrates is mortal," because it treats each as an unanalyzed atom. Predicate logic supplies the missing structure.

It introduces individual terms referring to objects, predicates expressing properties and relations, and quantifiers expressing generality. The universal quantifier asserts that a condition holds for every object in the domain. The existential quantifier asserts that it holds for at least one.

Standard translations follow recognizable patterns. "All S are P" becomes: for every x, if Sx then Px. Note the conditional. "Some S are P" becomes: there exists an x such that Sx and Px. Note the conjunction. Using a conjunction with a universal quantifier would assert that everything in the domain is an S, and using a conditional with an existential quantifier produces a claim that is trivially satisfied by any non-S. This asymmetry is one of the standard stumbling blocks and is worth memorizing.

Negation interacts with quantifiers according to rules paralleling De Morgan's laws. The negation of "everything is F" is "something is not F." The negation of "something is F" is "everything is not F." Translating a natural language negation therefore requires care about scope.

Quantifier order matters when more than one appears. "For every person there exists someone they admire" is a much weaker claim than "there exists someone whom every person admires." The first permits each person a different object of admiration; the second requires a single object common to all. This distinction, sometimes described as the difference between a universal outside an existential and the reverse, appears throughout mathematics. The definition of a limit and the definition of uniform continuity differ precisely in quantifier order, and the mathematical consequences of that difference are substantial.

Predicates may take multiple arguments, expressing relations rather than properties, and relations may be characterized by structural properties: reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, antisymmetry. An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, and it partitions a set into disjoint classes. A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. These structural facts recur throughout mathematics and computer science.

Predicate logic with identity adds an equality relation, permitting the expression of numerical claims such as "there is exactly one object with property F," which is rendered as the existence of an object with F together with the assertion that anything with F is identical to it.

NATURAL DEDUCTION AND PROOF STRUCTURE

A derivation system provides rules for constructing proofs step by step rather than checking every possible interpretation. Natural deduction, so named because its rules resemble ordinary reasoning, provides introduction and elimination rules for each connective.

Conjunction introduction allows deriving P and Q from P and Q separately; conjunction elimination allows deriving either conjunct from the conjunction. Conditional introduction is the important one: to prove if P then Q, assume P, derive Q under that assumption, and then discharge the assumption, concluding the conditional. This corresponds exactly to how conditional claims are argued in practice. Conditional elimination is modus ponens. Disjunction introduction allows deriving a disjunction from either disjunct; disjunction elimination is proof by cases, in which one shows that the desired conclusion follows from each disjunct separately. Negation introduction is reductio: assume P, derive a contradiction, conclude not-P.

Universal instantiation permits inferring a particular instance from a universal claim. Universal generalization permits inferring a universal claim from a claim proved about an arbitrary individual, provided the individual was genuinely arbitrary and no special assumptions about it were used. Existential instantiation introduces a temporary name for something known to exist, subject to the restriction that the name must be new. Existential generalization infers an existential claim from a particular instance.

The restrictions on the quantifier rules are not bureaucratic. Universal generalization from an individual about which something special was assumed is exactly the error of hasty generalization, formalized.

METHODS OF MATHEMATICAL PROOF

Deductive reasoning reaches its most developed form in mathematics, where several standard proof strategies recur.

Direct proof assumes the hypothesis and derives the conclusion through a chain of valid steps. To show that the sum of two even integers is even, write them as 2a and 2b, sum to get 2(a+b), and observe that this has the required form.

Proof by contraposition proves not-Q implies not-P instead of P implies Q, exploiting their equivalence. This is often easier when the negations are more tractable than the originals. To show that if n squared is even then n is even, prove instead that if n is odd then n squared is odd, which is a direct computation.

Proof by contradiction assumes the negation of the conclusion together with the premises and derives an impossibility. The classical proof that the square root of two is irrational assumes it can be written as a fully reduced fraction and shows that both numerator and denominator must then be even, contradicting the assumption that the fraction was reduced.

Proof by cases partitions the possibilities exhaustively and establishes the conclusion in each. The partition must genuinely cover every case, and this is where such proofs most often fail.

Proof by mathematical induction establishes a statement for all natural numbers by proving a base case and proving that the truth of the statement at an arbitrary value implies its truth at the next. Strong induction assumes the statement for all values up to a point rather than only the immediately preceding one, which is convenient for recursive structures that decompose in more than one step. Induction is a legitimate deductive method, not an instance of inductive reasoning in the philosophical sense; the terminological collision is unfortunate.

Proof by exhaustion checks every case when finitely many exist. Constructive existence proofs exhibit an object with the required property; non-constructive proofs establish that one must exist without producing it, for instance by contradiction or by the pigeonhole principle.

Counterexamples refute universal claims. A single instance suffices, which is why refuting a general claim is often far easier than establishing one.

LOGIC IN COMPUTATION AND ENGINEERING

Deductive machinery is directly embedded in computing. Boolean algebra, isomorphic to propositional logic, governs digital circuit design, where conjunction, disjunction, and negation correspond to AND, OR, and NOT gates. Circuit minimization is the simplification of Boolean expressions, and De Morgan's laws are used routinely to convert between gate types.

Program correctness is argued deductively. A precondition, a postcondition, and a loop invariant together allow a proof that a loop achieves its intended effect: the invariant holds before the loop, is preserved by each iteration, and together with the loop's exit condition implies the postcondition. Type systems are deductive systems in which a well-typed program constitutes a proof that certain classes of error cannot occur.

Satisfiability solving determines whether an assignment exists making a propositional formula true. Though the problem is computationally hard in the worst case, modern solvers handle enormous instances in practice and are used for hardware verification, scheduling, configuration, and planning.

Database query languages rest on predicate logic, with relational algebra operations corresponding to logical operations over relations. Logic programming makes deduction the execution model directly, with programs stated as facts and rules and computation performed by searching for derivations.

Formal verification proves that a design satisfies a specification for all possible inputs and states, rather than testing a sample. This is expensive and is reserved for components where failure is unacceptable: processor arithmetic units, cryptographic implementations, flight control, and railway interlocking.

THE LIMITS OF DEDUCTION

Deduction has boundaries that should be stated plainly.

It cannot establish premises. Every deductive chain terminates in assumptions justified by observation, definition, convention, or further argument of a non-deductive kind. Logic guarantees transmission of truth, not its origination.

It is not a complete account of good reasoning. Most practical decisions involve uncertainty, incomplete information, and conflicting objectives, where probabilistic and decision-theoretic reasoning are the appropriate tools. Treating an uncertain situation as though it admitted deductive certainty produces overconfidence.

Formal systems of sufficient expressive power have intrinsic limits. Any consistent formal system capable of expressing basic arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency. These results, established in the 1930s, do not undermine ordinary mathematics but they do rule out the ambition of reducing all mathematical truth to derivation within a single fixed system.

Real arguments are rarely presented in complete form. Enthymemes omit premises considered obvious, and much of the work of evaluating an argument consists of reconstructing what was left unstated. The charitable reconstruction, which supplies the most plausible missing premise rather than the weakest, is both fairer and more useful, because refuting a version no one holds accomplishes nothing.

Despite these limits, the discipline of deductive reasoning remains foundational. It supplies a standard against which looser forms of argument can be measured, a vocabulary for identifying exactly where an argument fails, and a set of transferable patterns that apply equally to mathematics, engineering, law, and everyday judgment. Its most valuable practical contribution may be the simple habit of separating two questions that are constantly conflated: does the conclusion follow, and are the premises true.


APPENDIX: A REFERENCE TABLE OF FORMS

The following forms recur often enough to be worth holding in memory. In each, P, Q, and R stand for arbitrary propositions.

Valid forms. Modus ponens: if P then Q; P; therefore Q. Modus tollens: if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Hypothetical syllogism: if P then Q; if Q then R; therefore if P then R. Disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. Constructive dilemma: if P then Q; if R then S; P or R; therefore Q or S. Simplification: P and Q; therefore P. Conjunction: P; Q; therefore P and Q. Addition: P; therefore P or Q. Contraposition: if P then Q is equivalent to if not Q then not P.

Invalid forms that resemble valid ones. Affirming the consequent: if P then Q; Q; therefore P. This fails because Q may have other sufficient causes. Denying the antecedent: if P then Q; not P; therefore not Q. This fails for the same reason. Illicit conversion: treating all P are Q as equivalent to all Q are P. Undistributed middle: all P are M; all Q are M; therefore all P are Q, which fails because the shared term M is never taken universally.

Equivalences worth recognizing on sight. De Morgan's laws: not (P and Q) is equivalent to (not P) or (not Q), and not (P or Q) is equivalent to (not P) and (not Q). Material implication: if P then Q is equivalent to (not P) or Q. Double negation: not not P is equivalent to P. Distribution: P and (Q or R) is equivalent to (P and Q) or (P and R). Exportation: if (P and Q) then R is equivalent to if P then (if Q then R).

Quantifier negations. The negation of "for all x, F(x)" is "there exists an x such that not F(x)". The negation of "there exists an x such that F(x)" is "for all x, not F(x)". Order matters: "for every person there is someone who knows them" is a far weaker claim than "there is someone who knows every person", and swapping the quantifiers silently is among the most common errors in informal argument.

APPENDIX: A CHECKLIST FOR EVALUATING AN ARGUMENT

First, identify the conclusion. This is often not the first sentence and is sometimes not stated at all. Second, identify the premises actually offered in support, distinguishing them from background remarks, restatements of the conclusion, and rhetorical framing. Third, reconstruct any suppressed premise the argument requires, choosing the most plausible version rather than the most convenient to attack. Fourth, ask whether the conclusion follows given the premises, holding their truth fixed for the moment; this isolates validity. Fifth, and only then, ask whether the premises are true, which is an empirical or definitional question rather than a logical one. Sixth, check for equivocation by confirming that each key term carries the same sense throughout. A term that shifts meaning between premises can make an invalid argument appear valid, and this is the single most common defect in arguments that survive casual inspection.
