GEOGRAPHY: A REFERENCE SURVEY OF EARTH, PLACE, AND HUMAN SETTLEMENT

Geography is the study of places and the relationships between people and their environments. It asks four recurring questions. Where is something located? Why is it located there rather than somewhere else? How does that location connect to other locations? And how do the physical and human characteristics of a place change over time? These questions apply equally to a mountain range, a shipping route, a language boundary, a rainfall pattern, and a neighborhood market.

Two broad branches organize the discipline. Physical geography studies natural systems: landforms, climate, water, soils, vegetation, and the processes that shape them. Human geography studies population, settlement, culture, economic activity, politics, and movement. The two branches are not separate. Farming depends on soil and rainfall. Cities grow where harbors, rivers, or passes make transport cheap. Flood risk depends both on river hydrology and on where people choose to build. Modern geography treats human and physical systems as coupled, and much of its practical value comes from that coupling.

A third strand, sometimes called technical geography, provides the tools: cartography, surveying, remote sensing, and geographic information systems. These tools have transformed the field. A question that once required a season of fieldwork can now be partially answered from satellite imagery, and a map that once took months to draw can be produced and revised in hours.

THE SHAPE AND MOTION OF THE EARTH

The Earth is very nearly a sphere, slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator because of rotation. This shape is called an oblate spheroid. Its mean radius is about 6,371 kilometers, its equatorial circumference about 40,075 kilometers, and its polar circumference slightly less. The flattening is small in relative terms, roughly one part in three hundred, but it matters for precise surveying, satellite orbits, and navigation.

The Earth rotates once on its axis approximately every 24 hours, producing day and night. It orbits the Sun once every 365.24 days, which is why calendars insert a leap day nearly every four years. The rotational axis is tilted about 23.4 degrees relative to the plane of the orbit. This tilt, not the varying distance from the Sun, causes the seasons. When the northern hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, it receives sunlight at a steeper angle and for more hours per day, producing summer, while the southern hemisphere simultaneously experiences winter.

Four dates mark the seasonal cycle. The June solstice and December solstice occur when the tilt is most directly toward or away from the Sun. The March and September equinoxes occur when the tilt is sideways to the Sun and day and night are approximately equal everywhere. Four lines of latitude follow from the geometry. The Tropic of Cancer at about 23.4 degrees north and the Tropic of Capricorn at about 23.4 degrees south mark the farthest points at which the Sun can appear directly overhead. The Arctic Circle at about 66.6 degrees north and the Antarctic Circle at the same southern latitude mark where at least one day per year has continuous daylight or continuous darkness.

COORDINATES, PROJECTIONS, AND MAPS

Any point on Earth can be described by two angles. Latitude measures the angle north or south of the equator, from 0 degrees at the equator to 90 degrees at each pole. Lines of constant latitude are called parallels; they run east to west and never meet. Longitude measures the angle east or west of a reference meridian, conventionally the meridian passing through Greenwich, England. Lines of constant longitude are called meridians; they run pole to pole and converge at the poles. One degree of latitude corresponds to roughly 111 kilometers everywhere. One degree of longitude corresponds to roughly 111 kilometers at the equator but shrinks toward zero at the poles.

Coordinates are commonly written in degrees, minutes, and seconds, or in decimal degrees. A location at 40 degrees 26 minutes 46 seconds north and 79 degrees 58 minutes 56 seconds west can also be written as 40.4461 north, -79.9822. Negative values conventionally indicate south latitude and west longitude.

A map projection is a mathematical rule for representing the curved surface of the Earth on a flat sheet. Every projection distorts something. A projection can preserve angles, or areas, or certain distances, but never all properties simultaneously. Conformal projections such as the Mercator preserve local angles and shapes, which makes them useful for navigation because a straight line on the map corresponds to a constant compass bearing. The cost is severe area distortion at high latitudes, which is why Greenland appears comparable in size to Africa on a Mercator map even though Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. Equal-area projections such as the Mollweide or the Gall-Peters preserve relative area at the cost of distorting shape. Compromise projections such as the Robinson or Winkel Tripel distort everything slightly rather than any one property severely, which suits general reference maps.

Scale expresses the ratio between distance on a map and distance on the ground. A scale of 1:50,000 means one centimeter on the map represents 50,000 centimeters, or half a kilometer, on the ground. Large-scale maps cover small areas in fine detail; small-scale maps cover large areas coarsely. The terminology is a frequent source of confusion because the intuitive sense of the words runs opposite to the technical one.

Thematic maps display a single variable across space: population density, average rainfall, election results, disease incidence, land cover. Choropleth maps shade administrative units by value. Isoline maps connect points of equal value, as contour lines connect points of equal elevation and isotherms connect points of equal temperature. Dot maps place one symbol per fixed quantity. Flow maps show movement of goods, people, or information along routes. Each form encodes a different assumption about how the underlying phenomenon is distributed, and choosing badly can mislead. A choropleth of raw counts, for example, mostly shows where people live rather than where the phenomenon is intense; rates or densities are usually more informative.

Geographic information systems store spatial data as layers that can be overlaid, queried, and analyzed. Vector data represent features as points, lines, and polygons with attached attributes. Raster data represent space as a grid of cells, each holding a value, which suits continuous surfaces such as elevation or temperature. Remote sensing supplies much of the raster input, using satellite and aerial sensors that record reflected and emitted radiation across visible, infrared, thermal, and microwave wavelengths. Because different surfaces reflect differently, imagery can distinguish water from vegetation, healthy crops from stressed crops, and built surfaces from bare soil.

PLATE TECTONICS AND THE STRUCTURE OF LANDFORMS

The Earth's interior is layered. A thin, rigid crust rests on the mantle, a thick shell of hot rock that flows very slowly over geological time. Beneath the mantle lies a liquid outer core and a solid inner core, both composed largely of iron and nickel. The crust together with the uppermost rigid mantle forms the lithosphere, which is broken into about a dozen major plates and many smaller ones.

These plates move relative to one another at rates of a few centimeters per year, comparable to the growth of fingernails. Three kinds of boundary result. At divergent boundaries, plates separate and new crust forms as magma rises; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the classic example, and the East African Rift shows the process beginning within a continent. At convergent boundaries, plates collide. Where oceanic crust meets continental crust, the denser oceanic plate subducts, producing deep trenches, volcanic arcs, and powerful earthquakes; the Andes and the Pacific Ring of Fire illustrate this. Where two continental plates collide, neither subducts easily and the crust thickens and buckles, raising ranges such as the Himalayas. At transform boundaries, plates slide past one another horizontally, as along the San Andreas Fault, producing earthquakes without much volcanism.

Plate tectonics explains a striking set of otherwise unrelated observations: the jigsaw fit of continental coastlines, matching fossil assemblages on now-separated continents, the age pattern of ocean floor rocks that grow older with distance from ridges, magnetic striping recorded in seafloor basalt, and the concentration of earthquakes and volcanoes along narrow belts. It is the organizing theory of the solid Earth in the same way that evolution organizes biology.

Weathering and erosion then sculpt the raised material. Physical weathering breaks rock apart mechanically through freeze-thaw cycles, thermal expansion, salt crystallization, and root growth. Chemical weathering alters rock composition through dissolution, oxidation, and hydrolysis; limestone dissolving in slightly acidic rainwater produces the caves, sinkholes, and dry valleys of karst landscapes. Erosion transports the loosened material by water, wind, ice, and gravity, and deposition lays it down elsewhere.

Rivers are the dominant agent of erosion on most land surfaces. A river system drains a catchment, or watershed, bounded by higher ground. In its upper course a river typically flows steeply through narrow V-shaped valleys, cutting downward. In its middle course the gradient eases and the channel begins to migrate sideways, forming meanders. In its lower course it deposits fine sediment across a floodplain and may build a delta where it enters standing water. Oxbow lakes, levees, terraces, and alluvial fans are all recognizable products of this progression.

Glaciers reshape landscapes in high latitudes and high altitudes. Moving ice carves U-shaped valleys, amphitheater-like cirques, sharp arêtes and horns, and deep fjords where valleys are later drowned by the sea. Glaciers also deposit unsorted debris as moraines and till. Much of northern Europe and North America carries the imprint of ice sheets that retreated only about eleven thousand years ago.

Coasts change constantly as waves, tides, and currents redistribute sediment. Wave energy erodes headlands into caves, arches, and stacks, while longshore drift moves sand along the shore to build spits and bars. Deltas, estuaries, barrier islands, dunes, and coral reefs each represent a different balance between sediment supply, wave energy, and biological activity.

Deserts are shaped by wind as well as by infrequent but intense water flow. Wind sorts sediment, leaving stony pavements where fine material has been removed and building dunes where it accumulates. Dune form depends on sand supply and wind direction: crescent-shaped barchans form where sand is limited and wind is unidirectional, while star dunes form where winds come from many directions.

CLIMATE, WEATHER, AND ATMOSPHERIC CIRCULATION

Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a moment; climate is the statistical summary of weather over decades. The distinction matters because a single cold winter says little about climate, while a shift in the thirty-year average of temperature or precipitation says a great deal.

Solar energy arrives unevenly. Near the equator, sunlight strikes nearly perpendicular and delivers more energy per unit area than at high latitudes, where the same beam spreads over a larger surface and passes through more atmosphere. This imbalance drives atmospheric and oceanic circulation, which continuously transport heat poleward.

The general circulation of the atmosphere organizes into cells. Warm air rises near the equator, releasing heavy rainfall in the intertropical convergence zone, then flows poleward aloft, cools, and descends around 30 degrees latitude in the Hadley cell. That descending air is dry, which is why most of the world's great deserts, including the Sahara, the Arabian, the Kalahari, and the Australian interior, cluster near those latitudes. Between roughly 30 and 60 degrees lies the Ferrel cell and the belt of prevailing westerlies, where mid-latitude weather is dominated by traveling low-pressure systems and fronts. Near the poles, cold dense air descends and flows equatorward as polar easterlies. The Coriolis effect, a consequence of the Earth's rotation, deflects moving air to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere, converting simple pressure-driven flow into the observed pattern of trade winds and westerlies.

Several factors control the climate of a particular place. Latitude sets the basic energy budget. Altitude lowers temperature by roughly six degrees Celsius per thousand meters of ascent, which is why equatorial mountains carry snow. Distance from the ocean determines continentality: coastal locations have moderated temperature ranges because water heats and cools slowly, while continental interiors swing widely between summer and winter. Ocean currents redistribute heat, so northwestern Europe is far warmer than its latitude alone would suggest, while coastal Peru and Namibia are cooled and dried by cold upwelling currents. Prevailing winds and mountain barriers create rain shadows, with heavy precipitation on the windward slope and aridity on the leeward side.

The Köppen system classifies climates into five main groups using temperature and precipitation thresholds. Tropical climates are hot year-round with abundant rain, subdivided into rainforest, monsoon, and savanna. Dry climates, both arid and semiarid, occur where evaporation exceeds precipitation. Temperate climates include Mediterranean patterns with dry summers, humid subtropical patterns with hot wet summers, and oceanic patterns with mild wet conditions year-round. Continental climates have warm summers and cold winters with significant seasonal snow. Polar climates include tundra, where the warmest month stays below ten degrees Celsius, and ice cap, where it stays below freezing. Highland climates are often listed separately because altitude compresses several zones into a short vertical distance.

Monsoons deserve separate mention because they govern the lives of billions. A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of prevailing winds driven by the differential heating of land and ocean. In summer, land heats faster than sea, air rises over the continent, and moist maritime air flows inland to produce heavy rainfall. In winter the pattern reverses and dry continental air flows seaward. South Asian agriculture, river flow, and food security depend on the timing and strength of this cycle.

BIOMES AND SOILS

A biome is a large ecological region defined by its dominant vegetation and characteristic climate. Tropical rainforests occur where warmth and rainfall are abundant year-round; they hold extraordinary biodiversity but often surprisingly poor soils, because nutrients are locked in the living biomass and rapidly recycled. Savannas experience a pronounced dry season and combine grasses with scattered trees, maintained partly by fire and grazing. Deserts support sparse, drought-adapted vegetation. Mediterranean shrublands occupy the dry-summer temperate zones and are fire-adapted. Temperate grasslands, called prairies, steppes, or pampas depending on the continent, developed deep fertile soils and have been widely converted to grain agriculture. Temperate deciduous forests shed leaves seasonally. The boreal forest, or taiga, forms a vast coniferous belt across northern Eurasia and North America. Tundra lies beyond the tree line, underlain in many places by permafrost.

Soil is the thin, biologically active layer where rock, water, air, and organic matter meet. It forms slowly from parent material through the combined influence of climate, organisms, topography, and time. A soil profile typically shows horizons: an organic surface layer, a topsoil rich in humus, a subsoil where clays and minerals accumulate, weathered parent material, and finally bedrock. Soil texture, determined by the proportions of sand, silt, and clay, controls drainage, aeration, and nutrient retention. Because soil forms over centuries and can be lost in a single storm, erosion control is one of the more consequential problems in land management.

WATER RESOURCES AND OCEANS

Water moves through a closed global cycle of evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, and runoff. About 97 percent of the world's water is saline ocean water. Of the small freshwater remainder, most is locked in ice caps and glaciers or held underground, leaving only a tiny fraction in the lakes, rivers, and soil moisture that are most accessible to human use.

Groundwater occupies pore spaces and fractures in rock. An aquifer is a body of rock permeable enough to yield useful quantities of water. The water table marks the upper surface of saturation. Where extraction exceeds recharge, water tables fall, wells must be deepened, land can subside, and coastal aquifers may suffer saltwater intrusion. Several of the world's major agricultural regions currently draw groundwater faster than it is replenished.

The ocean is a single connected body divided by convention into the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, and Arctic Oceans. The Pacific alone covers roughly a third of the planet's surface. Ocean floor topography includes continental shelves, continental slopes, abyssal plains, mid-ocean ridges, and trenches, the deepest of which exceed ten kilometers.

Surface currents are driven mainly by wind and organized into large rotating gyres, clockwise in the northern hemisphere and counterclockwise in the southern. The Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift carry warm water toward Europe; the California and Humboldt currents carry cold water equatorward along western coasts and support highly productive fisheries through upwelling. Deep circulation, sometimes called the thermohaline circulation or global conveyor, is driven by differences in temperature and salinity, and it turns over the deep ocean on a timescale of roughly a thousand years.

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation is the most important year-to-year climate fluctuation. During El Niño conditions, weakened trade winds allow warm water to spread eastward across the tropical Pacific, suppressing upwelling off South America and shifting rainfall patterns worldwide. La Niña conditions represent the opposite phase. The consequences reach far beyond the Pacific, influencing drought in Australia and southern Africa, flooding in Peru, and hurricane activity in the Atlantic.

POPULATION AND SETTLEMENT

World population passed one billion around 1800, two billion around 1927, four billion in 1974, and eight billion in 2022. Growth is not uniform. Total fertility rates have fallen sharply across most of Asia, Latin America, and Europe, while remaining higher in parts of sub-Saharan Africa. Where fertility falls below the replacement level of roughly 2.1 children per woman, populations eventually decline unless sustained by immigration.

The demographic transition model describes a common historical sequence. In stage one, both birth and death rates are high and population grows slowly. In stage two, death rates fall due to improved sanitation, nutrition, and medicine while birth rates remain high, producing rapid growth. In stage three, birth rates fall as urbanization, education, and changing economic roles reduce desired family size. In stage four, both rates are low and growth is slow. Some demographers add a fifth stage in which deaths exceed births. The model is descriptive rather than predictive, and countries have moved through it at very different speeds.

Population structure is often visualized with an age-sex pyramid. A wide base indicates high fertility and a young population, implying future demand for schools and jobs. A narrow base with a bulge in the middle or top indicates an aging population, implying pressure on pensions, healthcare, and labor supply. Dependency ratios compare the population outside working age to the population within it.

Migration is described by push and pull factors. Push factors include conflict, persecution, unemployment, environmental degradation, and disaster. Pull factors include employment, safety, education, family reunification, and political stability. Migration is internal when it occurs within a country, most commonly rural to urban, and international when it crosses borders. Remittances sent home by migrant workers exceed official development assistance for many countries.

Urbanization is one of the defining processes of the modern era. More than half of humanity now lives in urban areas, and the share continues to rise. Cities concentrate labor, capital, infrastructure, and ideas, generating productivity gains known as agglomeration economies. They also concentrate problems: congestion, air pollution, housing shortages, informal settlements without secure tenure or reliable services, and heat islands where built surfaces raise local temperatures several degrees above surrounding countryside.

Settlement patterns can be analyzed with several classical models. Central place theory explains why settlements of different sizes are spaced in a nested hierarchy, with many small villages providing everyday goods and few large cities providing specialized services. Concentric zone, sector, and multiple nuclei models describe internal urban structure. None fits any real city exactly, but each highlights a genuine organizing force: distance decay, transport corridors, or the tendency of specialized districts to form around several nodes rather than one.

ECONOMIC GEOGRAPHY AND RESOURCES

Economic activity is conventionally divided into sectors. The primary sector extracts raw materials through agriculture, forestry, fishing, and mining. The secondary sector manufactures goods. The tertiary sector provides services. A quaternary sector of information, research, and high-level decision-making is sometimes distinguished. As economies develop, employment typically shifts from primary to secondary and then to tertiary activity, though the path varies and some economies have industrialized only partially before shifting to services.

Agricultural systems vary along several axes: subsistence versus commercial, intensive versus extensive, arable versus pastoral, and irrigated versus rain-fed. Wet rice cultivation in monsoon Asia supports dense populations on small plots through intensive labor and water control. Extensive grain farming on the North American and Eurasian plains supports low population densities with high output per worker through mechanization. Plantation agriculture concentrates on export crops such as coffee, cocoa, rubber, palm oil, and bananas, often with a colonial history that continues to shape land ownership and trade relationships.

Industrial location depends on the relative weight of transport costs for inputs and outputs, labor costs and skills, energy availability, market access, agglomeration benefits, and policy. Heavy industry historically clustered near coal and iron. Modern electronics manufacturing clusters instead around skilled labor pools, supplier networks, and ports. Because components may cross borders many times before final assembly, container shipping and reliable customs procedures have become as important to industrial geography as raw material deposits once were.

Resources are classified as renewable or non-renewable, though the distinction depends on the rate of use. Solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, and sustainably managed forests are renewable. Fossil fuels and mineral ores are effectively non-renewable on human timescales. Fresh water and fish stocks are renewable in principle but can be depleted when extraction outpaces regeneration, a pattern often analyzed as a commons problem in which individually rational use produces collectively destructive outcomes.

Development is measured with several indicators. Gross domestic product per capita measures average economic output but ignores distribution, unpaid work, and environmental cost. The Human Development Index combines income with life expectancy and education. Additional indicators include infant mortality, literacy, access to clean water and sanitation, electrification rates, and measures of inequality such as the Gini coefficient. No single number captures development, and using several together usually gives a more honest picture than any one alone.

POLITICAL AND CULTURAL GEOGRAPHY

A state is a political entity with defined territory, a permanent population, a government, and recognized sovereignty. A nation is a group of people sharing culture, language, history, or identity. The two rarely coincide exactly. Nation-states where the two align closely are less common than multinational states containing several distinct groups, or stateless nations distributed across several countries.

Boundaries may follow physical features such as rivers and mountain crests, or they may be geometric lines drawn without reference to terrain or settlement, as across much of Africa and the Middle East. Boundaries drawn without regard to existing human geography have frequently produced enduring disputes, because they divide communities and combine groups with different histories under single administrations.

Geopolitics examines how geography shapes political power: control of straits and canals, access to warm-water ports, buffer zones, resource dependence, and the strategic value of particular territories. Supranational organizations, trade blocs, and treaty regimes create overlapping layers of authority above the state, while federalism and devolution create layers below it.

Cultural geography maps languages, religions, foodways, architecture, and other practices, and studies how they diffuse. Diffusion may be relocation diffusion, in which people carry a practice with them as they migrate, or expansion diffusion, in which a practice spreads outward from a hearth. Expansion diffusion can be contagious, spreading to nearby individuals, hierarchical, spreading first between large cities and then downward, or stimulus diffusion, in which an underlying idea spreads while its specific form is adapted. Language families such as Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan, Niger-Congo, Afro-Asiatic, and Austronesian record ancient patterns of migration and contact.

Globalization has intensified the exchange of goods, capital, people, and information. It has reduced some differences between places while sharpening others, since regions connect to global networks on very unequal terms. A port city with fiber-optic links and container terminals participates differently than an inland district reachable only by unpaved road.

HAZARDS, RISK, AND ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE

Natural hazards become disasters when they intersect with vulnerable populations. The same magnitude earthquake can cause a handful of deaths in a region with strict building codes and tens of thousands in a region without them. Risk is therefore usefully decomposed into hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, and only the second and third are amenable to policy.

Earthquakes result from sudden slip along faults. Magnitude scales are logarithmic, so each whole number represents roughly a thirty-fold increase in energy release. Ground shaking, liquefaction of saturated soils, landslides, and tsunamis are all secondary hazards. Volcanic hazards include lava flows, pyroclastic density currents, ashfall, and lahars, which are volcanic mudflows that can travel far down valleys long after an eruption. Tropical cyclones, called hurricanes or typhoons in different basins, combine wind, rainfall, and storm surge; surge is typically the deadliest component. Floods may be slow-onset river floods or rapid flash floods, and their severity depends heavily on land use, since paving and deforestation increase runoff. Droughts develop slowly and can be meteorological, agricultural, or hydrological depending on which deficit is measured.

Human activity has become a geological-scale force. Land conversion for agriculture and settlement is the leading driver of habitat loss. Combustion of fossil fuels has raised atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations well above their pre-industrial level, warming the lower atmosphere and ocean, raising sea level through thermal expansion and ice loss, acidifying seawater, and shifting precipitation patterns. Consequences are unevenly distributed: low-lying delta populations, small island states, and dryland agricultural regions face disproportionate exposure, often with the fewest resources for adaptation.

Responses fall into mitigation, which reduces the driver, and adaptation, which reduces the harm. Mitigation includes decarbonizing electricity, electrifying transport, improving efficiency, protecting forests, and changing agricultural practice. Adaptation includes flood defenses, drought-tolerant crops, early warning systems, revised building codes, managed retreat from indefensible coastlines, and urban design that reduces heat exposure. Both require spatial analysis, because the right intervention depends entirely on local physical and social conditions.

REGIONAL CHARACTER

Regions are conceptual devices for organizing spatial knowledge. A formal region is defined by uniformity in some characteristic, such as a wheat belt or a language area. A functional region is organized around a node and its zone of influence, such as a metropolitan commuting shed or a port's hinterland. A perceptual region exists mainly in shared understanding, with fuzzy boundaries that different people would draw differently.

Every continent presents distinctive combinations. Africa spans the Sahara, the Sahel, the Congo basin rainforest, the East African Rift and its highlands and great lakes, and the southern plateaus, with a young and rapidly urbanizing population. Asia contains the Himalayan and Tibetan high country, the great river plains of the Indus, Ganges, Mekong, Yangtze, and Yellow, the arid interior of Central Asia, the Siberian taiga, and the archipelagos of the southeast. Europe is characterized by a long indented coastline, navigable rivers, the North European Plain, the Alpine chain, and a dense network of historic cities. North America ranges from Arctic tundra through boreal forest and interior plains to desert southwest and humid southeast, with the Rockies and Appalachians framing the interior. South America is dominated by the Andes, the Amazon basin, the Brazilian highlands, the Pampas, and the Patagonian steppe. Australia is largely arid with population concentrated on the eastern and southwestern coasts, while the Pacific islands divide into high volcanic islands and low coral atolls with very different water resources and hazard exposure. Antarctica, governed by treaty and devoted to research, holds most of the world's fresh water as ice.

WHY GEOGRAPHIC THINKING MATTERS

Geographic reasoning contributes something distinctive: attention to location, scale, and connection. A problem examined at one scale may look entirely different at another. National averages can conceal severe regional disparities. A policy that succeeds in one place may fail in another because the underlying physical or social conditions differ. And phenomena that appear local are often produced elsewhere, as when consumption in one country drives deforestation in another.

The practical applications are broad. Epidemiologists map disease to identify sources and target response. Retailers use spatial analysis to site stores. Utilities plan networks around terrain and demand. Emergency managers model evacuation routes. Conservation planners design reserve networks that account for connectivity rather than treating each protected area in isolation. Urban planners weigh density, transport, and services against one another. In each case the underlying question is the same one geography has always asked: what is where, why is it there, and what difference does that make?
